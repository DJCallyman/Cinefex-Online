Weighing heavily on the production were methods for resolving the fate and presence of Leia, who remained a pivotal character. “We discussed creating a digital version of Carrie using existing footage,” recalled Roger Guyett. “We had filmed many additional scenes with Carrie in Episode VII, and J.J. proposed reviewing that material. The question then was how to integrate that footage with other shots. It turned out to be much more complicated than we imagined. In a scene, we didn’t want to simply cut to single shots of Carrie; we wanted to fully integrate her scenes with interesting staging, as though she was really there.” The process began by scanning unused takes of Fisher from Episodes VII and VIII, creating a library of footage. new Leia scenes. “There was a lot of in - depth production tracking,” commented ILM visual effects supervisor Pat Tubach. “We figured out which takes we could use by replicating camera angles and lighting. And while putting each shot in context for its narrative and visual qualities, we wanted to offer J.J. options. We were lucky in that J.J. was savvy about our process. Once we ex plained our parameters, he came up with cool ideas for shots that worked within those boundaries. For example, he knew he could pan or tilt his camera onto an existing frame, and so sometimes he restaged his framing using those techniques. In shots where the camera was moving, we had to be much more careful about what we could do on set.” In mobile Leia shots, ILM matchmoved archival footage and extended camera moves into production plates using motion control. “We resolved never to alter Carrie’s face,” stated Roger Guyett. “If Carrie was shot with a moving camera, we extracted the motion from that shot and programmed that move into our motion control camera. Resistance forces on Ajan Kloss include the diminutive alien Maz Kanata (voiced by Lupita Nyong'o). The philosophical creature, who had appeared in previous films in action-filled roles realized through design work with Neal Scanlan’s creature team and ILM digital animation, this time served a more sedentary role as an animatronic puppet. Built from the hips up, the animatronic was realized mostly as a self-contained, incamera character, which ILM assisted with subtle digital mouth articulation to cater to editorial revisions.
Senior animatronic designer Josh Lee takes the Maz puppet through its paces with puppet performer Claire Roi Harvey, who controlled Maz’s body motions in real time using an off-camera Xsens Moven motion capture suit. The full-body interface and radio controlled facial functions allowed the Scanlan team to imbue the Maz puppet with an expressive, real-time performance which J.J. Abrams coached as another member of his cast. A slider rig enabled Maz’s ambulatory motions.
Seeking further intel on First Order plans, astromech BB-8 and protocol droid C-3PO (Anthony Daniels) join Chewbacca, Poe, Rey and Finn on the desert planet of Pasaana, home to an Aki-Aki festival. The filmmakers staged festival scenes in the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan with caravans of tents, drifting kites, and 500 alien performers. ILM expanded the environment with digital terrain extensions built from Jordanian geology samples, lighting and lidar surveys, and combined them with digital crowd simulations of mid-ground and background Akis, cloth simulation kites, and brightly colored plumes of daytime fireworks.
For the Aki-Aki shoot, Neal Scanlan’s team sent a choreographer to Jordan in advance of the shoot and recruited Aki performers from local dance troops, theatre companies and military personnel. Creature designers then worked with costume designer Michael Kaplan’s wardrobe team to devise multiple tiers of creature masks with animatronic mechanisms for foreground and mid-ground creatures, plus additional tiers of pull-on latex masks and deep-background fabric masks that employed 3D-printing techniques. The practical creature work on location established important lighting and texture palettes that informed ILM’s visual effects enhancements.
First Order troopers pick up the Resistance trail on Pasaana and give chase to Rey and her friends, who commandeer desert skiffs, while treadspeeder drivers and their jet trooper pillion riders give chase on motorbike-like desert vehicles. The speeder chase made extensive use of digital backgrounds and full-CG virtual environments ILM constructed from Jordanian drone photography and location surveys. The production developed vehicle designs and previsualized action, which guided location shooting. ILM then assembled the speeder chase and animated treadspeeders and troopers using interactive lighting, laserbolts, dust and volume simulations amid desert terrain.
The filmmakers captured footage of Resistance performers riding Pasaana speeders on location in the harsh desert sun. To provide the nimble motions of the ramshackle craft, Dominic Tuohy’s special effects team designed and constructed a custom X/Y rig that transported two 30-foot-long speeder props along 100-foot tracks at right angles to each other. Waldo controls allowed Tuohy and his team to manipulate speeder motions, responding to the director’s action cues in real time. A stunt performer rehearses Resistance actions secured atop the X/Y rig via a body harness, surrounded by Jordanian terrain.
In addition to the Pasaana horde, Neal Scanlan’s team created 40 background aliens seen in a variety of settings, including the Resistance Base on Ajan Kloss and other planets. A featured creature, Klaud (Nick Kellington), received spontaneous applause upon its first on-set appearance. J.J. Abrams devised a backstory for the seeminglyconfused ambulatory slug, casting Klaud as a bemused traveler that Finn and Poe enlist on their intel-finding mission. Colin Jackman designed the creature, which the Scanlan team constructed as a two-piece latex suit, weighted to wobble. Josh Lee demonstrates Klaud’s face and tentacle articulation to Abrams and Oscar Isaacs.
Scanlan and creature concept designer Jake Lunt Davies discuss puppet functions for the droid, D-O, with J.J. Abrams first assistant director Tommy Gormley. The Scanlan design team conceived D-O as a progenitor to BB-8’s rolling sphere technology, which they brought to life with external rod controls and hidden stabilizer wheels.
The Pasaana speeder chase culminates with Resistance characters crashing into quicksand that deposits them in the lair of a Vexis worm. The production built the lair in England where the creature effects team threaded Vexis body sections and a polearm- mounted head. Neal Scanlan coaches Vexis performance on set at Pinewood Studios.
Rey approaches the Vexis worm, which she discovers to be injured, and uses her Force powers to calm and heal the savage beast. To stage Rey’s Vexis encounter, Daisy Ridley interacted with the Scanlan team’s enormous puppet. Important Looking Pirates then replicated the Vexis, preserving the puppet design and performance, and added extra fleshy details and deep-tissue articulation inside the creature’s maw. ILP used lidar scans, video and photos of the puppet to model the CG Vexis, and dressed the open mouth with stringing saliva.
After exiting the Vexis lair, Rey senses a presence on the Pasaana planet surface and separates from her friends to face Kylo Ren, who approaches from the far distance in his TIE silencer. J.J. Abrams staged the dramatic showdown, lightsaber versus TIE, with Daisy Ridley on location in Jordan. The production stunt and special effects team provided wire rigs and pyrotechnics. ILM animated the TIE silencer, its interactions displacing the desert surface, and assisted Rey’s maneuver, as the young Jedi vaulted Kylo’s TIE and used her laser sword to cripple Kylo’s ship.
On the frozen planet of Kijimi, the Resistance forces encounter a black market droid engineer, Babu Frik, who they discover to be a tiny alien. Neal Scanlan’s creature team created Babu as a rod puppet with extensive animatronic mechanisms packed into the creature’s tiny head. Green-suited puppeteers operated Babu using direct linkages to the puppet’s body, head and limbs, combined with a Waldo-driven facial performance system. Hybride provided rig and puppeteer removal, and created clean plates using extensive digital reconstruction of Babu’s workshop.
Babu’s animatronics included head mechanisms and mechanical hands that clipped on at the elbows for specific gestures and manual tasks. Green-suited puppeteers Wim Oppenheimer, Richard Garaghty, Alison McGowan, Damian Farrell and Claire Roi Harvey manipulate Babu’s rod controls. Vocal performer Shirley Henderson delivered Babu’s lines while operating the creature’s mouth via radio control.
A Sith Star Destroyer later obliterates Kijimi with a planet-killing cannon. The production commissioned 32Ten Studios to build a miniature planet, packed with explosive material, which ILM incorporated into its digital composite. 32Ten special effects technicians John Lanoue, Tory Fink and Robbie Clot join 32Ten special effects coordinator and senior pyrotechnician Geoff Heron to rig the Kijimi hemisphere.
For the Kijimi explosion shoot, 32Ten Studios prepared two hemispheres layered with miniature planet strata. Technicians hoisted each hemisphere above a trench in the stage floor where director of miniature photography Pat Sweeney aimed the camera upward, creating the illusion of a zero gravity detonation. The high-speed photography required a massive battery of lamps to achieve depth of field.
On the planet Kef Bir, Resistance fighters discover the half-sunken remains of the former Empire’s fallen battle station, Death Star II, amid a storm-tossed ocean where Kylo Ren appears for his final duel with Rey. As setting for a tumultuous lightsaber conflict, the production built a limb of the Death Star structure that projected like a pier into the Pinewood Studios Paddock Tank, where special effects bombarded performers with 24 water cannons. ILM captured panoramic wide views of the set. ILM effects artists then generated extensions, with water surface simulations, spray, splashing waves and glowing saber blades.
Rey escapes Kef Bir in Kylo’s TIE silencer, which she later destroys on the planet Ahchto. Abrams filmed Ahch-to surface scenes on a backlot set at Pinewood representing the ancient Jedi refuge, where special effects rigged a full-scale mockup of Kylo’s fighter to burn. Hybride created Ahch-to extensions, revisiting the Scottish highland location they had previously assisted during production of Episode VII, using photographic textures to represent a hitherto unseen corner of the island. Hybride effects artists detailed the burning TIE with additional pyrotechnic effects.
In Rey’s darkest hour, the Force ghost of Luke Skywalker (Mark Hamill) appears out of the flames of the burning TIE and returns Leia’s discarded lightsaber to his former student. Abrams filmed Hamill interacting with Daisy Ridley on the backlot set. Hybride generated composite treatments that layered Luke among TIE fighter flames, and re-created the series’ classic Force ghost look by applying subtle glows and selective translucency effects. These generated a spectral figure with an inner core that retained Hamill’s natural skin tones and reflected ambient lighting.
A First Order Resurgent-class Star Destroyer hovers above the planet Exegol, located in the galaxy’s Unknown Regions, secret location of an ancient temple where an insidious plot is underway to rejuvenate the fallen Emperor Palpatine, initiating a new galactic dominion led by the evil Sith. Exegol represented a nightmarish and unstable world, with a scorched barren surface and violent internal forces that Sith loyalists attempt to channel into their machinery. ILM developed planetary effects as digi-matte projections, with filaments of color representing volcanic activity and red Sith energy emissions.
In Exegol’s lower atmosphere, more than 1,000 Sith Star Destroyers fill the sky in a vast symmetrical formation, ominously awaiting orders from the Sith temple on the planet surface. ILM generated a new Sith Star Destroyer model that evoked the classic Imperial Star Destroyer, albeit larger, darker and more menacing, with a slash of red along the top of the hull indicating Sith livery, and a large planet-killer gun integrated into the craft’s belly. In the film’s finale, Resistance ships attack the Sith fleet, against overwhelming odds.
As the Resistance struggles in the battle above Exegol, an alliance of ships sympathetic to their cause rallies to their aid in the form of a Galaxy Fleet, with the Millennium Falcon at the fore. ILM generated the Galaxy Fleet using a staggering array of 16,000 ships, featuring cameos of many familiar vessels in the saga mythology – including prequels, Disney projects, vehicles from theme park rides and animated series – and many other ramshackle craft. Modelers and technical directors customized Galaxy Fleet ships using procedural systems to randomize paint schemes, and fin, wing and engine configurations.
Classic rebellion-era craft – A-wings, B-wings, X-wings, frigates, cruisers – and other members of the Galaxy Fleet dodge Star Destroyer laser bolts and giant destructive blasts of lightning that leap from fissures in Exegol’s surface. The aerial assault made extensive use of ILM’s Projectile Emission Workflow, which technical directors and digital artists used to color-code and choreograph laser fire. Lightning emissions featured procedural effects that compositors layered into shots to suggest Sith technology, which harnessed the elemental power of the planet.
As the battle rages, Finn, BB-8 and their allies mount an attack on the Star Destroyer command ship using a ground transportation ship to facilitate a raid of Resistance fighters astride Orbaks – longhaired alien equines native to Kef Bir – that charge across the giant spaceship’s hull. The production staged the Orbak raid using a full-scale recreation of a portion of the Star Destroyer hull. ILM then configured the production footage with its digital Star Destroyer model, positioning live-action elements in juxtapositions with the First Order command tower, and animated the ships and Resistance fighters above them.
Neal Scanlan’s team created Orbaks as prosthetic costumes worn by equine performers, working with animal handlers to acclimatize horses with soft fabric coverings that were tailored to fit. Orbak suits attached to saddles and covered horse tails, bodies and legs in long fur, with additional alien musculature crafted out of lightweight foam. The horses rode alongside special effects riding rigs on the Star Destroyer set at Cardington Studios, in England. ILM added Orbak head replacements, digi-doubles and environment extensions.
Meanwhile, on the Exegol planet surface, Rey enters a Sith temple and confronts her nemesis, the resurrected Sith Lord Palpatine (Ian McDiarmid). Neal Scanlan’s team created two stages of prosthetic makeup effects for Palpatine, with Stage One suggesting a less-evolved manifestation of the ancient Sith’s resurrection. Stage Two featured subtler coloration and makeup applications, implying improved health. The makeups strove to recapture the essence of the character previously created for McDiarmid by makeup artists Stuart Freeborn and Nick Dudman. Prosthetics combined established techniques of old-age stipple with modern silicone technology and ILM digital eye treatments.
Palpatine summons the power of the Sith to unleash a Force Storm that he hurls into the sky above Exogol, causing giant lightning bolts to strike the Galaxy Fleet, displacing ships including a Corellian and Virgillian ‘Bunkerbuster’ Corvette. ILM artists blocked out choreography of key vessels and sampled a library of procedural lightning elements to create dancing, jagged branches of plasma energy. Compositors then layered ships and worked with lighting artists to identify key moments in each shot, rendering and placing lightning bolts, which illuminated spacecraft and created volumetric lighting effects.
Rey faces Palpatine at his throne using the crossed lightsabers of Luke and Leia to repel the Sith Lord’s energy. The filmmakers referenced conceptual designer Ralph McQuarrie’s designs to create Palpatine’s throne within the Sith chamber set. ILM extended the environment to cathedral proportions. Lighting artists and technical directors lit the chamber, taking cues from on-set lighting effects, which they amplified to suggest off-camera light sources and energy emissions that flicker dangerously from fissures in the planet, illuminating drifting filaments of mist.
