Destroyed actor souls aren’t the only detriments to on - set seas of blue. The setup demands that directors of photography and their camera operators frame shots while depending on low - res previs or their imagi - nations to determine final compositions. For visual effects teams, massive bluescreen areas require the expensive and pains taking creation of detailed CG set extensions in postproduction. How much better it would be for all of the creatives — the director, actors, art department, camera and visual effects crews — if full environments could always be there, in camera. Unfortunately, movie schedules and budgets most often preclude the building of full sets or the transporting of huge production crews to locations — especially to those locations exotic enough to simulate alien worlds. Limitations of time and money are even more acute for television productions. It was exactly that problem George Lucas faced more than a decade ago when he W
was developing a live - action, episodic Star Wars - based television show. He’d made tremendous progress in the ‘what’ — 50 scripts were written and ready to go, and his art department had developed volumes of concepts and designs — when he ran into the wall of ‘where.’ “George recognized that one of the biggest challenges of doing Star Wars for television was the fact that it is set in a galaxy far, far away,” stated ILM visual effects supervisor Richard Bluff who, as head of ILM’s digimatte department at the time, joined Lucas’ TV project to develop environmental solutions. “How do you do eight episodes of Star Wars for a TV production, shooting an episode every ten days, while maintaining the scale and scope audiences would expect? The vast majority of environments would be alien environments, and were not places you could necessarily find on Earth. You can go to Tunisia for Tatooine, of course, but as you get to places like Dagobah, Kashyyk and other planets in the Star Wars realm, you run into the problem of having to do set extensions for every exterior location to give it an otherworldly feeling. So, I was asked to partner with George’s production team and figure out solutions to this key problem in getting that TV show off the ground.” Bluff and a small team ran a number of tests, none of which produced breakthroughs sufficient to solving the environments problem; and then, the slow - tracked production came to a complete halt when Disney bought Lucasfilm in 2012. Even so, Richard Bluff continued to think about how technology might bring Star Wars - scale environments to episodic television. In fall 2017, Disney and Lucasfilm reconsidered the feasibility of doing a live - action THE MANDALORIAN 2 / 36

George Lucas first explored the idea of a live-action, Star Wars-themed, episodic television show more than a decade ago, but was stymied by the challenge of bringing epic Star Wars scale to the small screen given the constraints of television shooting schedules and budgets. To keep the baby safe from ex-Imperial forces, the Mandalorian takes him to the outer rim planet of Sorgan in Episode 4. Sorgan village scenes were shot both on a backlot set and in the volume, with ILM and MPC providing background extensions. Ghost VFX digitally extended sets for later scenes in which Klatooinian bandits – portrayed by performers in Legacy Effects silicone pullover masks – steal the krill farmers’ harvest.
Shots inside a public house on Sorgan made use of a 1/8-scale Creation Consultants Inc. miniature, one of several miniatures the company built for scanning and photographic purposes, enabling CG teams to create LED screen content as quickly as possible. Creation Consultants Inc. model maker Josh Spooner at work on the pub miniature, which was made of natural materials to suggest a Native American type lodge.
Klatooinian bandits attack the rural village on Sorgan, armed with an AT-ST Imperial walker famously featured in Return of the Jedi. ILM had re-created the retro look of the AT-ST’s original stop-motion animation for Rogue One, which also influenced the look of AT-ST computer animation in The Mandalorian. Final animation was more refined, however, missing stop-motion’s characteristic artifacts and popping effects. ILM overhauled the AT-ST asset from Rogue One, giving it a new paint job and additional dressing to suggest the bandits had made it their own.
Episode 5 opens with the Mandalorian in the Razor Crest, trying to escape fire from an ex-Imperial ship. Space chase shots were captured within the volume, with moving star fields projected onto the LED screens. To simulate the battle in real time, director Dave Filoni stood next to the camera operator with video game controls, triggering laser blasts on screen with the press of a button.
His ship damaged and low on fuel after the space battle, the Mandalorian lands the Razor Crest on the planet of Tatooine for repairs. At a repair shop in Mos Eisley, industrious pit droids attend the spacecraft. Introduced in The Phantom Menace, the pit droids were among George Lucas’ favorite Star Wars characters. Given the context of the repair shop scene, Jon Favreau and Dave Filoni asked animators at Pixomondo and ILM to slightly tone down the pit droids’ comical demeanor from the earlier film.
While his ship is repaired, the Mandalorian walks the streets of Mos Eisley. ILM extended Mos Eisley sets designed and built by production designer Andrew Jones and his art department and construction crews on a backlot near Manhattan Beach Studios, the production’s base. Though a full 55% of set extensions were done in-camera, against the video wall, the remaining 45% – including this one – were traditional postproduction visual effects composites.
Looking for work in Mos Eisley, the Mandalorian meets a wannabe bounty hunter named Toro Calican (Jake Cannavale), and the pair sets off to track down the assassin, Fennec Shand (Ming-Na Wen), traversing the Tatooine desert on speederbikes. MPC built CG terrain using plates shot in Chile for the series’ second season, then animated speederbikes and Mandalorian and Toro digi-doubles in wide shots.
Closer shots of the characters astride the speederbikes were captured on a bluescreen stage at Manhattan Beach Studios, or on the backlot for shots requiring natural sunlight. Speederbikes were built by Studio Art and Technology, while special effects supervisor Roy Cancina and his crew provided the rigs upon which they sat for forward translation.
As Toro and Mando make their way across the desert, they spot two Banthas in the distance. Rather than model and animate a new Bantha for only two shots, Richard Bluff pulled never-before-seen dailies from A New Hope – shot by George Lucas – which featured an elephant dressed in Bantha costuming. Bluff duplicated the element and offset timings to create a shot of two Banthas, which ILM then refined.
Having engaged the help of the Ugnaught in facing ex-Imperial forces, the Mandalorian and his companion ride Blurrgs across Arvala terrain. Pixomondo animated shots of Blurrgs throughout the season. Early on, Jon Favreau enlisted animators to do a series of stop-motion tests. Only one stop-motion Blurrg shot made it into the final show, but Pixomondo used it as a style guide for its computer animation of the Blurrg.
To capture performers atop the creatures, production crews used a Blurrg buck mounted to a motion base, and positioned it in front of the video wall. Pixomondo developed Blurrg animations to drive the motion base, making sure those animations remained within the confines of what the physical buck and base could do.
Episode 6 has the Mandalorian attempting to break out a prisoner from a New Republic prison ship, where security droids patrol its corridors. Image Engine delivered a fight between Mando and the droids, starting with motion capture data of three stunt men standing in for the robots. Image Engine took a Doug Chiang concept and built it into a working droid asset capable of replicating the in-camera stunts. Image Engine also provided prison ship interior set extensions, working from photography and lidar scans of Andrew Jones’ practical prison ship set.
The season’s final episode sees the arrival of Imperial war criminal Moff Gideon (Giancarlo Esposito) and hundreds of Stormtroopers in the town square of Nevarro. There, troops turn their weapons on a pub where the Mandalorian and his cohorts are trapped. Throughout the season, Nevarro scenes were shot on a partial backlot set. LED screen environments for more expansive shots beyond the city were derived from scans, photography and photogrammetry of lava flows and volcanic rock in Hawaii.
