THE VISIONARIES CHRISTOPHER Nolan BY JODY DUNCAN Cinefex has been covering Christopher Nolan films since 2005’s Batman Begins, when the director moved from smaller, psychologically-driven fare such as Memento and Insomnia to big-time, epic filmmaking. Few directors have moved so confidentally in both the art and science of filmmaking as has Nolan, nor have the subjects of their films been as eclectic. So wide-ranging have been Nolan’s cinematic subjects, it is difficult to strictly characterize a ‘Christopher Nolan film’ — except to say that they are all intricate and complex, and made with a steady hand. Nolan talked to Cinefex for our 40th anniversary issue, and gave us his insights about his approach to making movies, and the vital role played by visual effects. By my calculations, you were only ten years old the first year Cinefex was published. What movies did you love as a kid? I was inspired by the blockbusters of the ‘70s, in particular George Lucas’ first Star Wars, as well as the Bond films of the time, which were done on a grand scale. At what age did you seriously start thinking about becoming a filmmaker? Right around that time, actually. I started making Super 8 films when I was seven or eight years old, but sometime around 12 or 13, I became aware of ‘director’ as a career. As a young guy watching Blade Runner and then Alien, I realized that even though they were completely different stories, with different casts, set in completely different worlds, the same mind — Ridley Scott’s mind — was behind them both. That made an impression on me. Your early films — Memento and Insomnia — didn’t have much in the way of visual effects in them, but Batman Begins and its sequels did. I’d always been interested in visual effects, and very much through Cinefex, to be honest, I’d kept in touch with everything that was going on with visual effects. But you’re right, there weren’t many visual effects in my first few films. Batman Begins was your first collaboration with visual effects supervisor Janek Sirrs. It was, and Janek is one of the great minds in visual effects. He was extremely helpful to me, and I engaged with him early on to discuss methodologies. We had a lot of very good philosophical discussions about the role of visual effects in films, and why certain visual effects of that time weren’t as convincing as they might be. His view was that you had to look at what the visual effects were being asked to do, and we factored that into our thinking about how we would do Batman Begins. I never wanted to ask visual effects to do things they wouldn’t be able to do convincingly. In the Batman films, and beyond, you shot practical miniatures for shots most directors would have done in CG, even back then. I’ve asked you about this before, and you’ve said you felt there was still a physicality to miniatures that was lacking in CG. No, I don’t think it’s changed at all. We’re still using miniatures all the time! Miniatures, forced perspective — they are fantastic tools, and they are the stock and trade of the visual effects profession. They are mature approaches, as well, and so you know what they can do. Of course, if you ask them to do something they can’t do, to get back to Janek’s point, they will be exposed as miniatures. It’s a matter of knowing their util - ity for a particular shot. Where does the all - CG approach become problematic, you think? There is still a difference between something that is photographed and something that is rendered from the computer, and the audience becomes increasingly so - phisticated in their ability to distinguish between them, even as the technology itself becomes more sophisticated. That’s why yesterday’s visual effects often don’t hold up. It’s an arms race that never ends. Today, it seems CG shots very often have a photographic base, at least. Increasingly so. Back when we were working on Batman Begins, I discussed with visual effects supervisor Paul Franklin and DNEG the idea of re - projecting photographic elements onto CG models to achieve photographic reality. But, at the time, producing 3D buildings and all the rest involved lidar scanning and fully rendering the textures — all of that. Ten years later, when I was having those conversations with them again, they’d gone back to using photographs as the basis for a lot of the CG renderings. Older techniques like photography became workable again because software had evolved to allow them to analyze what a camera could record. So the camera then becomes the more effective scanner. It’s an example of new technologies embracing older technologies. 4 / 11 CHRISTOPHER NOLAN
You again went with a practical approach for the TARS and CASE robots in Interstellar, which easily could have been computer generated. What was your thinking behind that? I really wanted a human being to puppeteer the robot and then use CG to take out the puppeteering rigs. I thought that was the best approach because it meant that the cast would have something to interact with. Another example of old - school techniques is your use of digital projectors to put views outside windows in Interstellar, replacing blue - and greenscreens. That was a form of rear projection, wasn’t it? It was. We used digital technology to make it more sophisticated, but that idea was based on rear projection, which is almost as old as cinema itself. It is now quite standard to do that, but at the time it was unusual. That leads me to what I call ‘rich man’s rear projection,’ which is the video wall — projecting environments on LED screens as a way of getting in - camera set extensions. You’ve never shied away from the lo gistical headaches of shooting even big action set pieces on location. Does the idea of shooting within a motion capture volume, against a video wall, appeal to you at all? You have to be open to any new technique, but it be comes a problem if it is promoted as a catchall that can replace certain aspects of production. For example, when digital cinema first came in, people said you wouldn’t have to light night shoots and things like that, which wasn’t the case at all. The video wall is the same thing. There was a little controversy recently when Martin Scorsese said that modern superhero movies are more like amusement park thrill rides than cinema. And it’s true that spectacle has become the order of the day — partly because there is a huge non - English - speaking market out there, but also partly because it is so easy to produce fireworks in CG. But the spectacle in your films never gets in the way of the hu man drama. What limiting principles do you observe to make sure your human story isn’t overshadowed by spectacle? The production methods I use tend to create certain limitations. The risk with animation, in general, is that if there are no limits imposed on it by the artist or the filmmaker, it can lose weight and become frivolous and superficial. Of course, there are great computer graphics artists and filmmakers who make sure it is always grounded. Whenever we’ve done fully CG shots we’ve tried to impose limitations that are analogous to how we would shoot that thing in the real world. When you approach things from a practical point of view, either on set or in the visual effects realm, there is weight to the images that I think helps ground the story. In looking at your movies, it seems to me the only one that couldn’t have been made without digital effects is Inception. I don’t know how those kaleidoscoping images could have been done in any other way. You may be right about that. Depending on how you mark the beginning, you’ve been at this for 20 or 30 years. How has the relationship between the director and visual effects changed in that time? I think it has changed a lot. I can only speak to my own process, but I spent years struggling to get back to the way directors had worked with visual effects in the 1970s — which was that the visual effects people were part of the production. It wasn’t a question of treating them as vendors, and using multiple vendors, and sending shots out to five different houses. We tried to get back to the idea that they are part of the production team — and we got there with DNEG and Paul Franklin and, now, Andrew Jackson. They are there during the whole shoot, they are filming things for me, filming things that go into the film, sometimes not even as visual effects. In a sense, they’re putting themselves out of a job, because they’re helping me find ways to achieve something in camera. They are very much a part of the production, working together with the director of photography and the physical effects crew. So in my films, we’ve had a rewarding move back to using one house for the visual effects, and that house being very much integrated into the production. That is most often not the case in other movies, though, and there have been periods when there was a lot of disconnect between visual effects and production. Why does that happen? I think it’s partly a function of the technology, because if everything is computer based, then you need to have renderfarms and offices, and that takes it away from production. It makes it more of an abstract relationship. 9 / 11 CHRISTOPHER NOLAN
When I cover big multiple - vendor shows, often the individual vendors have very little idea of what is actually happening in the film! They just have shots they’ve been awarded, and they do them, without any real context. Exactly. It becomes very disconnected, and very difficult to coordinate. I think, now, more and more filmmakers are getting back to embracing visual effects professionals as part of the production team. If you can find the right people, and they have the resources to get the job done, you get a much tighter and more productive relationship by working with a single visual effects house. Every art form experiences a golden age, and then it goes into decline. With boxoffice receipts down, with so much good content on television, with gaming and virtual reality experiences on the rise, are movies in decline? I think we’re in a very interesting place in filmmaking right now. I never embraced the digital revolution — I still shoot celluloid film — but what it has done is fairly magical. It means that a film can be shot anywhere you want and can look any way you want. There are no longer strictures at a technical level, or even at an aesthetic level. So I think we’re at a very exciting point in cinema. As far as other media and how that affects cinema, that’s a conversation that’s been going on for a very long time. Ultimately, film as a medium is infinitely versatile. Other media draw from it and influence it, but they don’t diminish it. It is simply the medium of people gathering together, watching a big screen, and having a story unfold. It is as vital as it’s ever been. So reports of its death have been greatly exaggerated? Director Christopher Nolan began making Super 8 films as a very young child, and was later inspired by the films of Ridley Scott – specifically, Blade Runner and Alien – to consider a career as a filmmaker. Nolan made his professional mark as a director with his early films, Memento and Insomnia. Nolan on location for the filming of 2017’s Dunkirk, his account of the famous civilian evacuation of thousands of stranded Allied troops in World War II. Nolan collaborated with special effects supervisor Scott Fisher, visual effects supervisor Andrew Jackson and a team at DNEG to bring the war drama’s action to the screen.
Whereas his early films had been small, character-driven pieces, Nolan moved into epic filmmaking with the Batman Trilogy. Batman Begins, released in 2005, eschewed campy elements in favor of a psychologically-driven storyline, and introduced a tortured, fully human Batman, played by Christian Bale. Though more reliant on visual effects – and visual effects supervisor Janek Sirrs – the spectacle in Batman Begins was achieved, as much as possible, in camera, always Nolan’s preference. Nolan directs Bale and Ken Watanabe in a scene set inside a Himalayan monastery where billionaire Bruce Wayne begins his physical and spiritual transformation into Batman.
The second film in Nolan’s Batman Trilogy, The Dark Knight, released in 2008, pitted Batman against the Joker (Heath Ledger). The screenplay, co-written by Nolan and his frequent collaborator and brother, Jonathan Nolan, explored the emotional depths of the Joker’s mad desire to ‘see the world burn.’ Chris Corbould orchestrated special effects, while visual effects supervisor Nick Davis shepherded 642 visual effects shots – a modest number given the scale of the film – through DNEG, Framestore and BUF Compagnie. Nolan and crew set up a shot in which Christian Bale, as Batman, prepares to leap from a high-rise in Hong Kong.
On the set of 2012’s The Dark Knight Rises, the final film in the Batman Trilogy, Nolan looks through the viewfinder of an IMAX camera. The Dark Knight had marked the first time the 70mm format had been used in a feature film, and Nolan continued to champion IMAX in several of his subsequent films as an integral part of his creative vision. DNEG supplied visual effects for The Dark Knight Rises – the most visually ambitious of the three films – under the stewardship of Paul Franklin.
Nolan and crew prepare to shoot a scene for Inception, released in 2010 and starring Leonardo DiCaprio. The film’s dreamlike images benefited from a particularly close collaboration between Paul Franklin, the team at DNEG and special effects supervisors Chris Corbould and Scott Fisher. Despite the mind-bending nature of the film’s visuals, Nolan still insisted on achieving effects practically whenever possible, preferring photography over rendered images. The notion of setting an action-adventure in a dreamworld had come to Nolan when he was in college, and he continued to tweak the Inception screenplay over a number of years.
The subject matter for Dunkirk captured Nolan’s imagination when he sailed the Channel from England to northern France. The arduous nature of the trip – made in peacetime, without the threat of bombs raining down – gave Nolan a greater appreciation for what the civilian fleet had endured during the 1940 evacuation of 400,000 Allied troops from the beach at Dunkirk. Nolan’s screenplay, which detailed the historical event from the perspectives of land, sea and air, was brought to vivid life by Scott Fisher’s practical effects on location, enhanced when necessary and in subtle fashion by DNEG.
