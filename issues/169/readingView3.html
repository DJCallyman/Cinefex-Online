THE VISIONARIES JAMES Cameron BY GRAHAM EDWARDS James Cameron cut his filmmaking teeth work ing for pop cinema icon Roger Corman, designing spaceships and supervising special effects on films such as Battle Beyond the Stars and John Carpenter’s Escape from New York. When he wrote and directed The Terminator in 1984, he used all the effects techniques he had learned to bring a certain killer cyborg, played by Arnold Schwarzenegger, to merciless life. With every film that followed, from Aliens to Avatar via the multi - Oscar - winning Titanic, Cameron not only continued to practice the art of cinematic illusions, but also actively urged it to evolve. In this exclusive interview, James Cameron recalls highlights from his early career, shares secrets about his current projects, and reflects on what it means to work as a filmmaker forever on the frontier. At what point in your life did you realize there was such a thing as visual effects? When I first watched a Ray Harryhausen film. I realized there was something extraor- J INTERVIEW BY GRAHAM EDWARDS
dinary being done, but I couldn’t quite understand how they were creating these images — and I couldn’t get any answers. How movies were made was like a big mystery castle on the hill. There weren’t ‘making of’ films or books like there are now, and there was almost nothing out there on visual effects. When did you move from just being curious to actually picking up a camera? It culminated for me when 2001: A Space Odyssey was released in ‘68. I had to know how they did that. Coincidentally, it’s one of the first films that had a major nuts and bolts book on how it was made, written by Jerome Agel. I read that book from cover to cover, probably five times, but the problem was the book raised a lot more questions than it answered. That was okay, because that was the beginning of my investigations. I started building models — of the actual ships from that movie — and figured out how to glue them onto a sheet of glass, put them in front of black velvet and light them from one direction so it looked like the sun in space. I figured out how to get my dad’s camera to run single - frame, and how to use models in the foreground to create depth in a real - world setting. I was 14 years old, and I was on fire. I was on my way. You used a lot of those same techniques in your early features. Aliens is packed to the rafters with almost every trick in the book. Right. One of our major shots, when they first enter the alien structure, was a foreground miniature. The Skotak brothers resurrected that idea, which was almost a dead technique by that point in time. They were masters at doing stuff in camera, which was great because I didn’t have any faith in opticals. I hated them. To me, opticals always looked phony, so we tried to use them as sparingly as possible. For the shot where the JAMES CAMERON 2 / 11
power loader falls into the airlock, we just tossed the model down there, glued it back together and tossed it again. No one would dream of doing things that way today. Some of those same tricks are evident in The Abyss, only now you’ve got a new weapon in your arsenal — Industrial Light & Magic’s digital animation, which you used to create the water tentacle. When I look back, it was a pretty scary decision to rest one of the most important sequences in the film on an untried technology. The stained - glass knight in Young Sherlock Holmes was the first CG character, but it was a floating group of rigid objects. No one had done a soft - surface character in a movie. We talked about all kinds of practical ways to do it using more conventional technology. I visualized an elaborate claymation process in which we would sculpt white clay, project VistaVision images of water onto the surface, generate an alpha and use refractive effects in an optical printer to create the transmission effect. Guess what? It would have worked. But it was a process that wasn’t going anywhere. It didn’t open a door to other possibilities. Unlike digital, which opened just about the biggest door imaginable. I was circling around the idea of CG, and had a bunch of tests done by people who were working in it. We committed to working with Dennis Muren at ILM, who said: “It’ll take a while, a lot of planning, and we’re going to have to throw resources at it. But we can do it.” His confidence gave me confidence. When The Abyss came out in ‘89, the movie itself barely broke even, but there was a huge response to that sequence. People just gasped. They were seeing something that looked real to them, but they couldn’t imagine how it had been done. JAMES CAMERON 3 / 11
The T -1000 in Terminator 2: Judgment Day feels like a natural progression from the water tentacle — all that flowing liquid. The liquid metal guy was part of my story for the original Terminator. But nobody knew how to do it. When I came to make the second film I went back to that story, only now we had the technology to do it — or we’d seen through the keyhole enough to believe it could work. Before I wrote the script, I called Dennis, and said, “Look, if I create a liquid metal guy who can run around and transform — can you do that?” There was a very long pause before he said, “I’ll call you back.” Next day, I’m sitting there poised to write the script, and Dennis calls and says yes. That was it. There was no test, no proof of concept, no test animation, nothing. It was literally, “All right, we’re gonna do this.” You were so enamored with the possibilities of the new technology that in 1993 you co - founded a visual effects company, Digital Domain, together with Stan Winston and Scott Ross. Yet Stan’s background was very much in practical creature effects. Stan was a visionary. He always said, “The artist is what’s important.” By that time, he’d done Terminator 2 with me, and Jurassic Park with Steven Spielberg, and he knew character design was going to increasingly happen in the computer, and the final execution of it would be a CG image. We both believed in that. It was only an issue of when it would happen. The name of the company was its own mission statement, really. We chose that because everyone was always talking about taking things into the ‘digital domain,’ like it was some sort of sacred transcendent thing. The early days of JAMES CAMERON 4 / 11
Digital Domain were radical — we used to fly a pirate flag over the building. There was a heyday of about eighteen months where we basically figured out how to do digital compositing, which we used in shows like Apollo 13 and True Lies. But I was unsatisfied with what we were doing. I didn’t want to just have a composite house. Stan and I wanted to create characters — it was the reason we started the company. I felt it was time for me to create something that I would direct that would really raise the bar. So, in ‘95, I wrote Avatar. The problem was the challenge was way too big. Everybody thought I was nuts. With all the flora and fauna, lighting the world, flying sequences, facial animation and speech, body musculature — there were so many tools that needed to be written across such a broad spectrum and we just didn’t think we could get there. I literally threw Avatar in a drawer and didn’t circle back to it until quite a bit later. By which time the state of the art had advanced considerably. Yeah, but when we decided to start Avatar, facial capture was limited to a marker - based system in a very small volume, capturing the face the same way they were capturing the body. That’s what they did on The Polar Express and Beowulf. It was very limited and horrible for the actors, with hours of gluing these little spheres of retro - reflective material on their faces. So I had this idea — I got it from watching music concerts, where the singer has a little boom that sticks out in front of their face to capture their voice. Why not do the same thing with a lightweight video camera and make the system image - based? Then, because you’re capturing the face as a separate data stream, the coarseness of what you require for body capture allows you to move the cameras back, so you can make the volume ten times bigger. That’s what we did, and we did it with Weta Dig- JAMES CAMERON 5 / 11
ital, because it fit strategically into what they had already done with Gollum. Were there any moments during the production of Avatar where you wondered whether you were really going to pull this off? It was a four - and - a - half - year project, and we were three - and - a - half years in before I saw one usable shot. We’d been working feverishly on this one sequence, where Neytiri sees Jake in the jungle and she’s about to shoot him, then the woodsprite lands on her bow and she stops. We’d been on it for about a month, iterating, iterating, iterating every day. Finally this shot came in, her extreme closeup. We were all pretty excited in the room. Nobody could fault it. I took it into my personal cutting room, and just ran the shot, and ran it, and ran it. Sitting there by myself, I thought: “We’ve done it! We’re going to pull this off!” And all this was just a year out from the film’s re lease in 2009. Right. Within a few days we had the whole sequence finaled, all seven shots, which left us a year to do the other 2,900 shots! I remember that moment specifically, when I knew it was going to work. It was in June 2008, just a few days before Stan Winston died. I got a call from John Rosengrant at Stan Winston Studio, and he told me I had to talk to Stan because he was really ill. But I didn’t put it together, because Stan had assured me he was in remission a few months earlier and that everything was fine. I called Stan up under the pretense of saying, “That thing we dreamed of, we’ve actually cracked it, and I want you to see it.” We talked a little bit, and at the end he said, “I love you.” The next day I went over to his house with the shots encrypted and loaded on my laptop. I sat waiting for about three hours, then Stan’s son came down and said, “It’s not going JAMES CAMERON 6 / 11
to happen today.” I went back the next morning, but Stan had already passed. So he never saw it, that thing we had originally set out to do. His team had been involved in the design of the characters for Avatar, but they were not involved in the execution and I hadn’t wanted Stan to see it until we had cracked the code. He would have loved it. That’s one hell of a bittersweet moment. Now, ten years after the release of the first film, you’re back on Pandora. What juicy new techniques are you using for the Avatar sequels? Nothing quite as revolutionary as using the head rig in performance capture, which is what really unlocked the possibility of the first Avatar. It’s all iterative, but some of the steps are fairly big. For example, we’ve spent the last six or seven years developing a much more advanced version of simulcam. It now has real - time depth — which doesn’t sound like much, but when you think about a character in a live - action environment, walking behind a table, coming forward, moving in front of a live - action character — we can now do all that in real time. And it’s true depth, derived from ster eoscopic data, not a multiplaning set of alphas being fed through an effects switcher. We’re using deep machine learning to drive the algorithm, so it actually learns where things are in the scene as you do rehearsals and refines itself over time. We use it for everything — every shot is a simulcam shot now. We build only the foreground portions of sets, and sometimes we don’t even have walls, we just put it all in using simulcam. It is truly amazing. So as you’re operating the camera — racking focus and all the rest of it — is the simulcam system able to pick up all those little subtleties? We’ve got everything slaved to the physical camera. If we pull focus to a live - action JAMES CAMERON 7 / 11
character, the CG character will go out of focus, in real time. There’s a perfect master - slave relationship between the servos on the stereoscopic high dynamic range Venice CineAlta camera. We’ve made huge leaps forward with that physical stereoscopic camera. I’m handholding an 11- axis motion control system on my shoulder, controlling the two Venice cameras and the two zoom lenses, and the whole thing weighs 35 pounds. And it’s all integrated with the simulcam. You can see that entire imaginary world through the viewfinder, but what about the actors? How do they connect with the alien characters who aren’t actually physically present on set? That’s actually the biggest impediment — having the actors know where to look and what to perform to. We spent a lot of money on an eyeline system that uses a cable - cam - type transport device to move a video monitor around. On that monitor is the video of an actor’s face — Sigourney Weaver or Sam Worthington or whoever — and there’s also a small speaker in there. The video can either be fed in real - time, or it can be precaptured. So the monitor essentially becomes a servo - actuated character’s head, moving around spatially as they would move at eight feet tall. You’ve said the Avatar sequels have sequences with the Na’vi underwater. Tell us how you’re approaching those. We had two underwater shots in the first movie. One was when Jake jumps over a waterfall and comes to the surface, which we captured with a stunt guy named Ilram Choi in a wire harness. The other was where Jake and Neytiri are swimming through a lake at night. That was two of our troop performers lying belly - down on office chairs being rolled JAMES CAMERON 8 / 11
across the volume while they did a frog - kick. I said: “We’re not going to shoot all the underwater sequences in the next four films on office chairs! We’re going to do it in water, for real.” Which meant you needed an underwater performance capture system. Actually, what we needed was a capture system that could work underwater, at the surface of the water, and transition through the interface. It was that interface transition that turned out to be the most difficult part. We wound up with two volumes stacked one on top of the other, with the air - water interface between the two volumes. It took around three years to develop and it was hideously difficult, but the first few days we used it we were looking at each other slack - jawed with how well it was working. The actors were down there holding their breath with 14 -16 video cameras shooting them from all directions, and I could direct them completely dry through my virtual camera. I could see exactly where they were in the underwater world, and the creatures too — because the Na’vi have close interactions with their water creatures. The final sequences won’t have a fantasy operatic feel — they’ll feel very gritty. The beauty of the images will come from the sense that they are absolutely real. The actors were holding their breath? No scuba gear? They couldn’t be on scuba. Any air bubbles would act as false marker dots and the noise filtering couldn’t keep up with it. The underwater camera guys — there were three of them — were breath - hold specialists, and we had the world’s best breath - hold diving teacher, Kirk Krack, working with us. All the actors went through breath - hold training. Kate Winslet got to the point where she could hold her breath for seven - and - a - half min- JAMES CAMERON 9 / 11
utes. That was in static apnea, just floating doing nothing, but she would regularly do scenes that went two to three minutes underwater, and Sigourney was regularly doing two - minute scenes. From The Terminator and Aliens to Avatar and beyond — a lot has changed in the visual effects business since you started your career. Oh my god, there is almost nothing that was done then that’s still in practice today. Nothing, except maybe the glass of the lens that bends the light rays. The substrate on which the image is written — if there is a real - world image being created — is an electronic sensor. Young effects people don’t even know what an optical printer is — literally. Film is a thing of the past. Everything is digital. Given the capabilities of digital, is there anything a filmmaker can imagine now that can’t be rendered convincingly with visual effects? Right now, I can’t imagine anything we couldn’t do if we put our minds to it. It really gets down to a cost - benefit analysis — how much is that image worth to you? How does it advance the narrative cause of your movie? That’s where you get into a lot of subjective issues because, basically, you’re trying to justify an expenditure for an image. But, if we can imagine it and communicate it to the artists, we can do it. If you’re confident you can create anything, how do you avoid getting seduced by all the endless possibilities that opens up? The wise approach is to choose your battles and not take on too much. You’ve got to be rigorous about that. If you’re just doing an image for an image’s sake — and I think we’ve all seen those films — you’ll fail. I’m sure there are mystified filmmakers out there JAMES CAMERON 10 / 11
thinking: “But we’re showing them wonders! Why are we failing?” Well, it’s because your wonders didn’t serve your characters and your story, and didn’t create the emotional impact you wanted. But, the more beautiful the images are, the more you want to hang onto them in the edit. Sometimes taking stuff out is the most painful part of the process. Technology like your new simulcam setup is un doubtedly sophisticated, but at the same time it can bring a kind of simplicity to the set, a transparency that allows the filmmakers and actors to just do their thing. Yeah, there’s a transparency for the director, all in the pursuit of creating a naturalistic and humanistic reality for images that would otherwise be completely impossible. I can’t make it completely transparent to the actors, but I can make it as easy for them as possible, meaning they’ve got somebody there to perform to, who’s feeding them emotional energy to which they can respond. I want an actor to be able to improvise in the moment and get something back from a character that’s nine feet tall. That’s my goal, and I’d say I succeed nine out of ten times. Every once in a while something just defeats me. But that’s how you know you’re at the frontier — which is where I like to be. JAMES CAMERON 11 / 11

Inspired by 2001: A Space Odyssey and the films of Ray Harryhausen, Cameron began making his own movies as a teenager. After designing and supervising special effects for Roger Corman at New World Pictures, he forged ahead as a director in his own right. His early features made creative use of traditional effects techniques such as rear projection and hanging miniatures. No stranger to innovation, Cameron pushed the development of digital effects for The Abyss, and elevated performance capture and virtual production to new heights with Avatar.
Arnold Schwarzenegger rehearses as the cyborg assassin in The Terminator. Gene Warren Jr., co-founder of Fantasy II, handled miniature effects, including spectacular battle scenes set in a future Los Angeles and a gritty showdown between Sarah Connor (Linda Hamilton) and her robot nemesis. These latter scenes featured a stop-motion Terminator constructed by Doug Beswick. Stan Winston Studio fashioned full-scale Terminator puppets and props, and designed the iconic robot makeup worn by Schwarzenegger.

Cameron wrote and directed the sequel to Ridley Scott’s classic sci-fi horror movie, Alien. Scaling up the approach he had used for The Terminator, Cameron combined designs by conceptual artist Syd Mead with miniature effects by Bob and Dennis Skotak and a host of slavering monsters created by Stan Winston Studio, including a fourteen-foot alien queen realized both at full size and as a quarter-scale puppet.

Building on digital techniques developed for The Abyss, Industrial Light & Magic breathed life into the T-1000 (Robert Patrick), a shapeshifting robot made from liquid metal. Working at the frontier of what was possible, a small team of artists developed techniques that would form the foundations of modern digital effects. Stan Winston and his team worked alongside their visual effects counterparts, devising extraordinary puppets and makeups to give the T-1000 and his Terminator opponent (Arnold Schwarzenegger) physical presence on set.
An experienced diver, Cameron channeled his marine experience into the making of the multi-Oscar-winning Titanic, and even descended 12,500 feet in a submersible to capture footage of the sunken ship in her final resting place on the seabed.
