THE VISIONARIES GEORGE LUCAS BY JOE FORDHAM Merriam - Webster Thesaurus offers many alternatives to the word ‘visionary’ — inspired, imaginative, creative, inventive, insightful, ingenious, enterprising, innovative, perceptive, intuitive, far - sighted. Any of the above could easily be applied to filmmaker George Lucas. The softly - spoken son of a Modesto, California, stationery storeowner famously sprang to fame among a gang of cine - literate iconoclasts — Francis Coppola, Martin Scor - sese, John Milius, Brian De Palma and Steven Spielberg — who burst onto the 1970s filmmaking scene and challenged Hollywood culture. Unlike his contemporaries, Lucas was an introvert and automobile enthusiast. After a near - fatal car crash before his high school graduation, he emerged seemingly transformed, ignited by passions for New Wave and Japanese cinema, and the writings of philosopher Joseph Campbell. Postgraduate studies led to his friendship with Coppola, who produced Lucas’ first feature, the dystopian science fiction THX- 1138, and American Graffiti, a warm - hearted coming - of - age drama — the first Lucasfilm Limited production — whose critical and financial success gave Lucas the springboard to make Star Wars. Arguably, we wouldn’t be here without George Lucas who, since his retirement in 2012, has remained both a private man and an iconic figure. For the first time in 15 years, Lucas agreed to join Cinefex for a look at the future of filmmaking. You stated many years ago that movies were not your first love, growing up in Modesto. Clearly that changed — but what movies made an impression on you back then, or in later years? Like anybody growing up, I had lots of different interests — not like some of my friends who wanted to make movies from the very beginning. I wasn’t really involved. I was more interested in cars. Building cars and building things: buildings, architecture, that sort of thing. In the early years, it was films like Ben - Hur, Stagecoach and Forbidden Planet. Later, I became very interested in abstract films that were made by Canyon Cinema and the abstract filmmakers of the ‘50s and ‘60s. Then eventually my interest in film deepened. Once I went to film school, and was exposed to a much wider range of films than ever before. I particularly en joyed the films from the Canadian Film Board and I liked GEORGE LUCAS 2 / 11
Kurosawa films and Godard. I then started going to film festivals and seeing films that were made by my peers. What fascinated you about cars? Can you tell us about your Fiat Autobianchi, and what do you remember of your accident, June 12, 1962? Simply put, I just liked the speed, I loved the sensation of going fast. I don’t think it’s that unusual for young people. I started when I was about twelve, because I grew up on a ranch. My Fiat was very small, sort of like a Fiat 500 now. I worked at a foreign car service at the time and hot - rodded it. It didn’t have a top and I had put a roll bar in it. So, it was highly modified. That was my main interest until I was in an accident where I rolled it several times, and was nearly killed. I can’t remember anything about it except, you know, one moment was driving and the next waking up in a hospital. Do you see that moment as the beginning of your hero’s journey — to quote Joseph Campbell’s book? I didn’t know about hero’s journey at that point. But it was the beginning of me giving up on cars and going to college, which was definitely a turning point. Joseph Campbell’s book affected my thinking. I first read his book in an anthropology class. Between that book, and the class, and my other social interests, I became very fascinated with history, anthropology, so ciology, and psychology. So, it definitely changed my thinking and that’s what I was going to do. I was putting my effort into it and was going to get my degree in an thropology. And then fate changed it to go to USC and learn about film. I was shocked that you could go to college to learn how to make movies. When I got to USC, I realized very quickly that I was very good at it and I loved GEORGE LUCAS 3 / 11
it. This was it for me. I had found my passion. Steven Spielberg once marveled how you have ‘all the best toys’ — of course, you had to invent many of those toys for your films. How was it, reviewing the state of the art in 1974, on the brink of Star Wars? Right before I did Star Wars, and when I was working on Star Wars, the visual effects business was really part of the studios, but it had fallen down as not being that important. The few, crazy people who were interested … most of them ended up coming to work for me at ILM and the others left at the studios were basically matte painters. In my opinion, at the time the state of the art was very misunderstood. That’s the best way to put it. The film industry hadn’t really changed much since the beginning in the 1800s. So, there was a very low technological ceiling when you got to the world of visual effects. Basically, we were doing the same things with matte paintings and with the same restrictions, the same camera tricks and things. But they all had huge restrictions. It was a brick wall you couldn’t go through to do things. The process of making films then was cumbersome, at best. Editing was slow and burdensome. It was all very 19th century. You know, sprockets, celluloid and gears — it didn’t just feel antiquated to me; it was old, a hundred years old, in fact. I think that caused a lot of the problems with being able to actually tell stories. And that’s what it really comes down to in the end, all the techniques of telling stories really depend on technology. And the technology is really determined by the people who are doing it and how involved they are in helping to create the environment that you can actually explore new ideas which, obviously, the studios weren’t that interested in doing. nancing technology. Once filmmakers took over in the ‘70s, there was more interest in the technology and trying to get rid of some of the problems that kept us from telling the stories we wanted to tell. So, my analogy is to the fact that pre - digital technology was like making frescos. That really was the way it was. The change came when we began developing and leveraging digital technology. Once we shifted to digital systems, we could do anything. I’m not sure there’s anything now that can’t be rendered with digital effects. The glass ceiling is broken and we can go anywhere we want and tell any kind of story we want. That doesn’t mean they’re going to be good, it just means that we have the ability to do it. For a lot of those years, you just couldn’t do things. Either it cost too much money, or you didn’t have time, so the industry focused on more easily - produced movies about people sitting in rooms talking. And the idea of ten thousand people in an epic, which they used to do in the beginning, when extras and everything were very cheap, you just couldn’t do anymore. If you look at the films of the ‘50s,‘60s and ‘70s, they’re all very contained. You do have Lawrence of Arabia and some other movies with epic scale, but they were so big and challenging to make they became very cumbersome and had fallen out of favor. For a young filmmaker growing up in this era of dexterous modern filmmaking techniques, what are the most important criteria to master? Filmmaking is about ideas. It’s not about techniques. It’s about stories and how you tell those stories. It shouldn’t be confused with the technology of making movies. Yes, it’s important to understand the technol ogy, just as it is for a painter to understand the GEORGE LUCAS 5 / 11
use of paintbrush, paint, and how to mix the right colors. Those kinds of technologies have always been important; but it’s really not what creating a painting is. A painting is about ideas. All digital filmmaking is, or cameras, or editing equipment or anything like that — they are just tools. Tools to help you tell a story. It doesn’t tell the story any more than a pen writes a script. What it does is determine the fluidity of the storytelling in the case of digital as opposed to analog technology. That doesn’t mean analog films are inferior, or boring. It just means if you’re making films using the new technology, certain things are easier, which in turn means that you can focus your efforts on other aspects of the work. Whenever you work in a medium, whether working in black and white or color, or whether it’s film or digital, the things that happen, especially younger filmmakers, is that when a new technology comes around, a lot of peo ple get very excited about it and they forget about the stories and the ideas. That’s always been the case. Some of the first 3D movies were really horrible. Some of the first color films were horrible. First sound films were horrible. I’m a big fan of silent movies, so I can remember when people decried the end of film when sound came in. Which of course wasn’t the case. It was just another tool and another way to tell a story. It broadens the palette the filmmaker has to share their vision. We’ve seen the pendulum swing recently in a call for more physical assets in filmmaking. Is this an issue, or is it always going to be a filmmaker’s personal style choice? I’m not sure this is true across the board. That’s an artifact of the Internet. Without GEORGE LUCAS 6 / 11
the Internet, you wouldn’t know about that sort of thing. And there are people, just as there were those who said that we should not put sound on film, that want to have movies the way they used to be. It’s always been an issue. But I think a filmmaker can use whatever tools he or she needs to tell the story one wants to tell. There aren’t absolute rules. Filmmaking isn’t a process devoid of inspiration or creativity. The rules are the things that tell you how things work, but you don’t have to follow them if you don’t want to. I grew up in the age of Godard, which was a heresy for editing, but it sure played with new ways of telling stories. Your peers have built on new filmmaking resources — Jim Cameron is blazing his own trails, Steven Spielberg jumped into VR, Martin Scorsese has his gangster epic. There’s also exciting work being done by a new generation. Is there still a collegiate spirit, sharing works in progress? Most of those technological resources are ones that I developed and implemented out of necessity for what I was trying to achieve onscreen. Later, Jim became a partner in the effort to develop digital technology. He wanted to do 3D, I wanted it for editing, Steven was interested in doing VR. All those things are of value as tools for storytelling. It’s still a little early to figure out how immersive entertainment is going to fit into the business model of filmmaking, though it continues to evolve. So many of us knew each other in the beginning because we went to student film festivals. We liked to meet other filmmakers and learn what they were doing. When we were in school, we were very lucky. We were closed out of the film industry at that time and on a road all to ourselves. Back then, you had to practically be related to the moguls GEORGE LUCAS 7 / 11
who owned the studios to get a film made. With the march of time, the moguls were retiring and studios shifted under ownership of corporate conglomerates. There were about 10 good years somewhere in there where we had a little bit of freedom. There is a mantra that ‘visual effects should always support the story,’ not vice versa — however, critics have cynically implied this is no longer the case. Visual effects should ultimately support story, of course. People think we create visual effects for fun and then figure out what to do around it. We develop and use it in order to tell a story — so, for example, you can have Yoda be in a lightsaber fight. The effects of digital technology enhance the ability to tell a story. People have always initially complained about filmmakers using new technology. Widescreen was overdone, 3D was overdone. At the advent of a new innovation or technique, everyone starts using it. When digital began, it was overdone. They forgot about the story. Of course, there are people who say too many special effects destroys the story, but that’s not true. Some people complain that some effects - heavy films aren’t real films; I’m not inclined to believe that. I’m a firm believer that art is in the eye of the beholder. If it moves you, it’s art. In that regard, everything is art and nothing is art. You are the opposite of a cynic; you’ve always invested in infrastructure — from Sprocket Systems, THX, Skywalker Sound, LucasArts, to your philanthropy. What drives you to cultivate these resources? I pioneered digital technology and helped cultivate infrastructure to support it. I realized you had to have a whole food chain. We also had to develop technology to digitize the image so it could be worked on and then somehow get it recorded back to film. We invested heavily, developing a production viable system and ultimately sold it to Arriflex so they could share the innovation with the industry at large. Then color timing, the Young Indiana Jones series was the thing that allowed us to do digital effects color timing. The shift to digital progressed. It was the same with THX sound systems in theaters. Theater sound hadn’t changed in 50 years and could have been much better quality. Filmmakers wanted nothing more than to have film reach theaters with the same level of quality as an answer print. Like anything else, something may have worked fine 100 years ago, but we live in the age of computers now. We now have the Internet at our fingertips — but remember: at the beginning it was slow, and painful. Certainly, the nuts and bolts of filmmaking have changed fundamentally in 40 years. What do you foresee as the most exciting developments? Are the buzzwords of high frame - rates, stereo imaging and virtual reality leading us toward a new definition of cinema? We grew up in the age when cinema took over as a very important part of our culture. It is important to learn, regardless of the medium in use, that whether by pictorial art, theater, or anything else, it’s about the story. At USC, you needed a liberal arts foundation because learning how to make movies is easy, what to make movies about is hard. There isn’t a new definition of cinema, as it’s still storytelling using the moving image. People have a tendency to move things like video games and VR into that cate- GEORGE LUCAS 9 / 11
gory, but those mediums still have a long way to go in terms of the grammar of how they tell stories. Talented people around the world are working on it but it’s the challenge you face whenever new technology gets a foothold. With each technology, a language of its own develops as the technology develops. It’s difficult to cut closeups in VR — in time, people will figure out how to do that. When movies first started using closeups, people fled the theaters. VR and games still have a way to go, but it’s all a work in progress. It isn’t enough to just have a high frame - rate, or the latest technology, because they don’t change the way of telling a story. It’s sometimes just a different visual language than people are used to seeing. Technology has the ability to fundamentally change cinema; but a new camera, or other tools have little effect on the quality of the story being told. One of your most intriguing personal pursuits is your upcoming Museum of Narrative Art. What do you hope visitors will gain from a visit there when it opens in 2021? The Lucas Museum of Narrative Art is about visual storytelling. It’s really about understanding how a society is built using visual images to create a common language for the rules and regulations of a society. Patrons of the arts emerged, such as the Church and secular society, and decided what the stories of their culture were. Aspirations of people of a particular society went on to inform a common belief system. Common belief systems were valued throughout various cultures and eras; visuals of who their gods, heroes and history are. Filmmaker George Lucas lines up a shot in Kauai, Hawaii, a location that doubled for 1936 Peru, in the opening of the 1981 Lucasfilm production, Raiders of the Lost Ark. Lucas’ first collaboration with his long-time colleague, director Steven Spielberg, introduced audiences to a character inspired by Saturday matinée Republic serials, embodying Lucas’ fascinations with archeology, mythology and cinema history. The film combined classic cinema storytelling with action, stunts and state-of-the-art technology, and won Oscars in art direction, sound, film editing and visual effects produced at Industrial Light & Magic.
Six years earlier, in a desert canyon location in Tunisia, Lucas awaits a camera setup with actor Alec Guinness, veteran performer of many David Lean productions, who brought gravitas to the role of Jedi master Obi-Wan Kenobi in Star Wars. Twentieth Century Fox took a gamble on Lucas’ vision for a space opera that combined elements of fairy tale, science fiction and Samurai adventure. The film required considerable investment in new techniques and hardware that catapulted effects technology into a new era, and introduced a mythology to a new generation.
Lucas reteamed with Spielberg for the follow-up to Raiders, the ominously titled Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom, with a Gunga Din-style adventure set in India, 1935. The climax of the movie, filmed on location in Sri Lanka, featured a standoff between protagonists on a rope bridge strung 300 feet above a crocodile-infested gorge. The production constructed its own bridge, employing a team of British engineers who erected the seemingly flimsy construction, which special effects supervisor George Gibbs and his team rigged to collapse on camera.
After a hiatus from directing, Lucas delved back into the Star Wars saga, building on technological advances that ILM had nurtured since the completion of the first trilogy for Star Wars: Episode I – The Phantom Menace. The shoot involved locations in Tunisia, Italy and a production base at the U.K.’s then-fledgling Leavesden Studios, a recently converted World War II aerodrome and Rolls-Royce aeronautics plant. The production intermingled traditional filmmaking techniques, miniatures and creature effects with emerging digital technology, including a prototype digital camera system.
Star Wars: Episode III – Revenge of the Sith closed the circle in Lucas’ two trilogies, bringing the Machiavellian tale of intergalactic intrigue to full froth, with Imperial corruption ravaging the galaxy, and Luke Skywalker’s father, Anakin (Hayden Christensen), completing his transformation into the fearsome Darth Vader. Lucas’s tale brought to fruition story notes that he had first developed in the early 1970s, and required ILM to conjure exotic worlds and beloved key characters, including the diminutive Jedi master Yoda, rendered as digital animation.
Shooting for Star Wars: Episode II – Attack of the Clones included a return to Luke Skywalker’s moisture farm homestead on the planet Tatooine, built on the site of the original Tunisian Star Wars location, where Lucas directed scenes with protocol droid C-3PO (Anthony Daniels). The loquacious droid and his chirpy cylindrical astromech sidekick, R2-D2, served as the Greek chorus of the Star Wars universe, sole observers of the tumultuous Skywalker family saga – inspired by characters seen in Akira Kurosawa’s 1958 film, The Hidden Fortress.
