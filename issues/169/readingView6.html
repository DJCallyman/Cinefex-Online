THE Visionaries ROBERT Zemeckis BY GRAHAM EDWARDS ay the name ‘Robert Zemeckis’ and many people will immediately think of films rich in innovative camera tricks and visual effects, from the Back to the Future trilogy and Who Framed Roger Rabbit to The Walk and beyond. Some will recall the Oscar - winning Forrest Gump, in which Zemeckis used cutting - edge techniques in support of an emotional, character - driven story. Others see the filmmaker as a champion of performance capture and computer animation, as evidenced in films such as The Polar Express. We invited Robert Zemeckis to tell us what he really thinks about visual effects, and explain how he uses them to illuminate character, advance narrative, and entertain audiences with his own unique brand of cinematic spectacle. Have you always been drawn to films featuring visual effects? Well, first and foremost, I’ve always felt the real power of cinema is the audience identifying with the dramatic arc of a character. But I also think cinema can provide spectacle in a way no other art form can. Actually, the difference between a dramatic movie S INTERVIEW BY GRAHAM EDWARDS
and a documentary is spectacle, by which I mean showing the audience the world in a way that they can’t see in reality. So visual effects are a means by which you can create that spectacle? Yeah. I’ll even tell you what my favorite visual effect is — the closeup. It’s something that doesn’t exist in real life. It only exists in cinema. The closeup has always been the most powerful storytelling tool we have. It’s a visual effect, and it’s spectacle. Most films have closeups, but they don’t all have flying DeLoreans. It’s funny — everyone thinks the first Back to the Future was a giant visual effects movie. But there were only 30 visual effects shots and a lot of those were lightning effects with a locked - off camera, because that’s all you could do back then. You went a lot further with the Back to the Future sequels. All those moving split - screen shots where the actors are playing multiple roles, for example. That’s complicated stuff — why not just stick with the locked - off camera? Because anyone could do that! Here’s the thing — there’s no research and development departments in any of the studios, not that I know of. So, when you have an opportunity to make a sequel to a giant hit movie, I feel one of the responsibilities of the filmmaker is to use that open checkbook to invent or develop a new tool. In this case, a motion control system you could use to capture multiple passes on set. Right. When Bob Gale and I decided we were going to do the Back to the Future sequels, we came up with this story that involved time travel duplication. dolly, and they came up with the VistaGlide. We needed that to tell the story, and at the same time we were able to advance the art form. The moving camera is something of a constant in your films. What’s the appeal? One of the director’s jobs is to let the audience know where the story is taking place — what the landscape is, where an important window or door or piece of furniture is. One of the most efficient ways to give the audience that information is to create an elegant, moving camera shot. I say ‘elegant,’ because you don’t want to call attention to it — it should all be subliminal. Although, audiences are used to being aware of the camera nowadays, so I guess I’m dating myself by saying I don’t want to give away the technique. I don’t think that matters anymore, actually. Give us an idea of the thought process behind one of these shots, and why sometimes you might need visual effects to achieve it. I’ll use Allied as an example. There’s a scene in the movie where Max and Marianne make love for the first time in the car. We’ve seen scenes like it a million times, so how are we going to do this in a way that’s fresh and innovative and compelling and emotional? What can we do here that isn’t going to be just the same old shit? That’s what I presented to the visual effects team. You ended up with the camera circling the characters inside the car while a sandstorm rages outside. Real actors, CG car interior, digital sandstorm, right? Yeah. The idea was that it would be more cinematic if the camera is constantly moving. We did four takes, then we were done. So Brad Pitt and Marion Cotillard could immerse themselves in a single continuous performance. Exactly. We didn’t wear them out. You talked about using effects to create spectacle. In Cast Away, there’s over 450 visual effects shots, but most of them are environment fixes that go by unnoticed by the audience. Did you anticipate using visual effects so heavily in that film? Yeah, because we had to create a completely isolated island surrounded by an impenetrable reef — that’s the setting the story demanded. I don’t even know if something like that exists in the world, let alone if you could get accessibility and permission to shoot there. There’s a shot where Tom Hanks’ character picks up a couple of boxes off the beach and looks everywhere around, and the camera shows the audience exactly what his dilemma is. There’s another one where he climbs to the top of the peak on the island and we see the entire surroundings. They’re both important for the story, because you have to see there’s no other is lands, no boats, nothing on the horizon. All that had to be digitally painted. But I don’t know how the language of cinema changed specifically. What I do just evolves over time. What’s the biggest evolutionary step you’ve experienced in your career? The real breakthrough is digital cinema. That’s changed things all the way around. It’s unbelievable how breathtakingly different it is from when I started. Getting rid of film, mercifully, has been one of the more liberating things for me — not having to worry about this 100 - year - old photochemical thing. Allowing cameras to be so small. Having an image that can be manipulated and recomposed. Is it a different experience for you as a director, using virtual tools instead of physical ones? No. It’s no different moving a device with a lens on it than it is to move a camera virtually. They’re the same to me because, at the end of the day, the final product is shadows on the screen. It doesn’t matter how the shadows are created. There are some people who say, “I miss the feel of film in the editing room.” To my mind, that means nothing. It’s the two cuts together and how they tell the story, that’s what matters to me. You’re not afraid to use technology to help you tell a story. But are you secretly a techno - geek at heart? No, no, I don’t even have a smartphone! I know how everything on a camera works, but I can’t break one down. I know the entire process of how you composite a digital ROBERT ZEMECKIS 5 / 7
shot, but I can’t write code. What does that make me? An appreciator of technology, maybe? In the early years you made many films with Ken Ralston as your visual effects supervisor. More recently, you’ve worked consistently with Kevin Baillie. How important is that kind of continuity for you? Obviously you have a creative shorthand, which is just magnificent. Ken and Kevin come from that very rare school of visual effects where they never say “no.” I think I would be very frustrated working with people who say that something can’t be done. Both those guys are very open to seeing what can be done, and that energizes everybody. You’re currently working on an adaptation of Roald Dahl’s The Witches. Tell us about your visual approach to this classic story. The Witches is my first foray into melding live - action and 3D photoreal animation together. I haven’t done a film like this since Who Framed Roger Rabbit. It’s so much easier now! You can do an elaborate story and make it look completely real, and you don’t have to spend billions of dollars to do it. Do the capabilities of modern visual effects free you up as a filmmaker? Oh, yeah. When we moved the camera in Roger Rab bit, it turned the animation world upside - down. Every one said it was impossible. It took somebody like myself, who had never made an animated film, to say, “Let’s just do it!” As far as The Witches is concerned, no one would even bring that up. more, although we do use a Technodolly here and there. We can track any shot now, put anything in it, take anything out. Speaking of Roger Rabbit, Richard Williams once remarked that everything worked better on that film when he broke the rules. That’s why it was great to have somebody like Richard as my director of animation, because he loved breaking the rules. Every time we wanted to do something, he just said, “Yes, let’s go for it!” What about you? Are you a bit of an anarchist at heart? I wouldn’t say I set out to break the rules. It’s more that I like to challenge myself. That’s what keeps it interesting. I can’t imagine what it would be like to just wake up every day and do an eye - level, in - the - camera kind of movie. I think it’s an important responsibility for filmmakers to entertain the audience when they make a film — and when I say “entertain,” I don’t just mean making them laugh. I mean showing them an image that maybe they haven’t seen before, or do it in a way that makes them feel something in a new and different way. Does it take courage to constantly challenge yourself like that? Some people might call it insanity! But yeah, whatever courage you need to take risks, that’s what a filmmaker has to have. Because every time you make a movie you have to step into a ring of fear. You can’t be suicidal, but you have to be able to say, “This may or may not work — let’s just see what happens.” This is a fear - based art form, and if you’re a person who can’t abide that feeling, you know, you should probably get another job.
