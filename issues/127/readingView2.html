By Jody Duncan

In 2004, director Roland Emmerich read a screenplay by John Orloff titled Soul of the Age, a ‘who-wrote-it’ tale of suspense and political intrigue that proffered a theory as to how masterful plays penned by the learned and cultured Earl of Oxford came to be credited to an uneducated and all-but-illiterate actor named William Shakespeare. Devoid of alien invasions, giant lizards or end-of-the-world cataclysms — all of which had been the stuff of Emmerich’s movies for more than a decade — Soul of the Age promised to be a quieter, smaller and more contemplative film.

After years of bringing epic-scale extravaganzas to the screen, Emmerich found the modest script, with its subtle, intriguing storyline, enormously appealing. “It was a story I’d had no clue about,” admitted Emmerich. “I didn’t know there was a serious question that Shakespeare had written those plays. So I talked to John Orloff and did some research on the Internet, and I discovered how much heated debate there is about this subject. Exploring that debate appealed to me; but I also recognized, from the beginning, that the script would benefit from some significant

rewriting. That first script was relatively simple, and a little like Amadeus in that it was about the nature of genius and jealousy between rivals, which was not a story I wanted to do so much. So I asked John Orloff if he was open to giving it a major rewrite, and he was.”

E
mmerich was unable to get the project off the ground at the time; but five years later, while embroiled in production of the massively scaled and budgeted 2012, his thoughts returned longingly to the modest charms of Soul of the Age. “Roland really wanted to do a smaller movie as his next project,” noted longtime Emmerich collaborator Volker Engel who, with Uncharted Territory partner Marc Weigert, would serve as executive producer and visual effects supervisor on the retitled Anonymous. “He’d always loved this story; and so he started talking to Sony, and they figured out how to do it on a fairly small budget. Part of that plan was to make it in Germany, and do all of the effects with Uncharted Territory setting up a unit there.”

Those effects would be dedicated to creating utterly realistic and historically accurate late-16th-century environments. “Early on,” observed Marc Weigert, “we decided to create Elizabethan England as it really was, rather than shoot locations that weren’t authentic. For example, we had scenes set at Whitehall Palace. There is still a Whitehall Palace in England, but it’s different because the one that was there in the 16th and 17th centuries burnt down in 1698. If we had shot that Whitehall

Palace, it wouldn’t have looked anything like it did in those days. Roland loved the idea of re-creating authentic locations of the period. For the first time in modern history, people were going to see what it would have looked like to ride up to Whitehall Palace during the 16th century.”

With a greenlight from Sony Pictures — and while still in postproduction on 2012 — Emmerich had his first Anonymous meeting with Weigert, Engel, producer Larry Franco and director of photography Anna Foerster. The filmmakers sat around a table and talked through the screenplay, with Emmerich laying out his ideas as to how the film could be shot simply and inexpensively. “At first,” recalled Volker Engel, “Roland thought that a lot of the dialogue scenes outside some of the mansions could be set in front of hedges, with a visual effects shot only here and there. But it didn’t turn out that way. It wound up with almost every exterior scene being shot in front of greenscreen, with just a little hedge or set piece in the foreground. Still, Roland was pretty dead on with his estimated count of about 300 shots.”

After that initial meeting, Engel remained stateside to oversee the final weeks of postproduction on 2012, while Marc Weigert traveled to England to do a pre-shoot, photographing background plates and stills of scenic areas and Elizabethan-era structures for Anonymous. “I went through villages and towns all over England that still had buildings from the 16th and 17th centuries,” Weigert explained, “and photographed the hell out of them, using a Canon 5D camera mounted to the top of

a 15-foot-tall telescopic tower with a remote arm to shoot rooftops. I came back with 50,000 photographs for purposes of photogrammetry. I also shot background plates with a RED camera. For example, we knew there were going to be scenes at the house belonging to William Cecil, the main villain of the movie. So we shot a background plate of a beautiful English mansion. We wound up having to add a lot to it, but that plate was the basis of what became Cecil’s house.”

Only days after the premiere of 2012 in Germany, Weigert and Engel set up their Uncharted Territory operation from scratch at Studio Babelsberg in Potsdam, about 30 minutes outside Berlin, working with a crew of four CG artists. “We started work a week before even the art department moved in,” remarked Volker Engel, “which is unheard of. And then, when they moved in, they were in the same building with us so that we could work very closely together.” That initial Uncharted Territory crew — which, in addition to Engel and Weigert, included modeling and lighting lead André Cantarel, animation lead Conrad Murrey, lighting TD Robert Freitag and visual effects art director Greg Strasz — stayed with the project until the end, nearly 11 months later. Engel and Weigert brought on additional 2D and 3D artists and TDs in the interim, winding up with a crew of 28 from all over the globe, plus eight support people, to produce the show’s 300 effects shots.

C
ollaboration with Eyeon would be a crucial factor in setting up the in-house effects team. Uncharted Territory had a longstanding relationship with key
personnel at Eyeon, and the show’s compositing supervisor, Rony Soussan, had worked with the company previously. For both of those reasons, Weigert and Engel decided to use Eyeon’s Digital Fusion as their compositing software, and relied on Eyeon to develop special tools for that package, as needed. “We made a deal where Eyeon agreed to give us direct support and actually program features into the software that we needed specifically for this show,” explained Weigert. “And then, when the movie was over, they could put those features into the next version of the software. We’d done the same thing with the particle system software on 2012. It’s a good deal for them; and it is the best way for us to set things up, because we don’t have a team of ten programmers on staff.”

Visual effects shots broke down into three basic categories. “We had big, sweeping establishing shots of Elizabethan London,” said Volker Engel, “which we knew from the get-go would have to be all-CG. We were also going to be extending partial sets built and shot on studio soundstages. Even the exteriors would be shot on stage, for the most part, lit to look like overcast outside. Finally, we would have shots involving the plates that Marc shot in England, either as stills or as running footage, putting our actors and additional set pieces into those images in about a dozen shots.”

First on the visual effects agenda was building the entire sprawling city of London in 3D. “I thought it was important that the audience understand and see the true size of Elizabethan London,” Roland Emmerich commented, “which was the

biggest city in the world at that time. In the past, other movies of this kind have been very enclosed. But it was always my idea to use visual effects to open up the movie. At the very beginning, I wanted to do London as models, but that would have been too expensive; and so we wound up doing it CG.”

The Uncharted Territory team started by scanning period maps of the city to produce high-resolution versions that would guide them as to the layout of the 16th — and 17th — century buildings. “We built all of our CG buildings,” explained Marc Weigert, “and populated London with those buildings, based on the original maps. So it was very accurate. Obviously, there was some directorial license, but we wound up with something that was very close to how London looked before most of it burnt down in 1666.”

The tens of thousands of buildings that made up London of that period precluded Uncharted Territory’s modeling each one by hand, individually; and so Weigert and Engel developed an efficient system — which they dubbed the ‘ogel’ system, ‘Lego’ spelled backwards — as a fast, modular approach to building the myriad structures, starting with photographs Weigert had captured during his pre-shoot in England.

Study of the photographs of the Elizabethan-era houses revealed that only three basic types existed. Modest structures were either comprised entirely of stone, or were half-stone, half-timber, while large mansions were elaborate works of masonry. Work- ing within those parameters, the Uncharted Territory team created ten

different ground floors, each featuring either stone or timber or a combination of the two, and each with a different number and configuration of doors and windows. The team then modeled ten different second stories, and ten different roofs. “At that point,” said Weigert “we had ten times ten times ten, so we could create 1,000 different buildings from those pieces by mixing and matching them. Of course, all of those different parts had to be built very carefully so that they would fit together.” The dearth of clean right angles and the overall crooked lines of the buildings made precise fits all the more problematic. “I’d always assumed that old buildings like that had started out straight, and then become crooked through the years. But they were actually built that way, with weirdly crooked timber beams and so on. So we built the houses accurately, with squared lines; and then we went through and warped them and otherwise modified them to make them crooked. It was important that they not look symmetrical and architecturally correct, and that was a daily fight in dealing with computer models.”

The small crew continued work on the 3D city as production commenced, with Emmerich shooting scenes in studio annex space situated in an old East German train factory. “It was retrofitted,” said Weigert, “but there were girders and beams every- where, and no catwalks. There was even still an old slogan on the walls that said, ‘Comrade — Think About the 5-Year Plan.’”

A
mong the biggest sets built at the studio annex was one representing the
Rose and, later in the film, the Globe — the two theatrical venues featured in the story. “Nobody had ever really built the Globe for previous movies,” stated Roland Emmerich. “Even for Shakespeare In Love, they’d only built a couple of walls. They didn’t build the whole round theater. But we built all of that, and I think you feel that in our shots. We had a lot of Steadicam shots in the theater, and we shot a lot of wide lenses, too. So you would really see everything, which is unusual for these kinds of movies.” The studio annex included an outdoor space large enough to enable construction crews to build the theater exterior, as well, with a street in front. Crews built about two-thirds of the circumference of the circular theater, up to all three stories, but — as a cost-saving measure — at 80-percent scale.

A modular system for other interiors enabled production to move walls and change room sizes as needed. “As a result,” said Volker Engel, “we didn’t have to do many extensions of interior sets. We also didn’t have to do many window shots for the movie. There were usually paintings outside the set windows, or there would be rain so the view was obscured. Roland is always very good about doing whatever he can on set to save visual effects money and resources.”

One of the few interior extensions was for a high-angle shot inside Westminster Cathedral, the setting of the funeral for William Cecil (David Thewlis). “We took a digital still photo inside Salisbury Cathedral for this,” said Engel, “looking down into the church. We did a three-dimensionalization of the still photo to make it look like there was a camera crane move. Our three-dimensionalization technique was

comparable to projecting an image. If you project a slide onto a few boxes, and those boxes coincide with what’s in the slide, you get a 3D effect. It doesn’t work in an unlimited way, of course, but if you just move your camera slightly, you can get some parallax and it really feels as if it is 3D. We did the same thing, splitting our still photo into several different parts and mapping those onto 3D planes, which allowed us to do a 3D camera move. It was a nice technique that we used quite a bit because it was a cheap way to get a shot. We were able to go pretty far with that technique.”

In contrast, the partial exterior sets built on stage required extensive CG — and fully 3D — augmentation. “The main stage had 360 degrees of greenscreen,” Engel explained, “and then we’d have one or two arches or other set pieces on the stage. Whether the scene was on the London Bridge, or approaching Whitehall Palace, we would have this little bit of set in the middle of the greenscreen stage floor, and then we’d extend everything around it. All of the dialogue scenes on the London Bridge, for example, were shot on a very small section of the bridge itself, with a few one-story structures. Everything around it was extension.” Another example is a scene in which the older Earl of Oxford (Rhys Ifans) picks up his son from the Tower of London. “The actors performed that scene against greenscreen, and we filled in the entire environ- ment around them.”

While Engel remained with Emmerich on the main unit shoot at the studio, Weigert directed a splinter unit shoot for exteriors that could not be captured on

stage, such as shots of characters on horses. “We shot characters riding right-to-left, left-to-right, toward camera and away from camera,” explained Weigert, “against a very large green- screen — 120 feet by 25 feet high — that we built outside.”

A
ll-CG wide establishing shots required the most man-hours, and two of them — a 35-second-long top view of 20,000 people walking across the frozen Thames to witness the funeral procession for Queen Elizabeth (Vanessa Redgrave), and an aerial view of the Tower of London — were started at the very beginning of the project and completed only at the end of postproduction, nearly a year later. “We really wanted to make these big establishing shots spectacular,” said Weigert. “We didn’t want to take any shortcuts. To be honest, we wound up going a lit- tle bit overboard with the detail in these shots — but they are both really cool!”

Initially, the funeral procession scene had been set on the flowing river, with peo- ple arriving via boats; but Emmerich — having learned that the Thames froze over from time to time in the Elizabethan era — decided to re-stage it with throngs of mourners actually walking across the waterway. “I saw these paintings of the frozen river,” Em- merich recalled, “and I thought, ‘We just have to do something with the frozen Thames!’ Also, it coincided with an idea I had about a certain dramatic point in the movie, where something terrible is revealed — my idea was that everything in the movie would begin to freeze at that point. Everything gets very

quiet and cold, and the funeral on the frozen Thames fit that very well.”

Effects artists created the 20,000 mourners using Massive. “We shot stills of 50 to 60 extras,” said Marc Weigert, “and from that built 14 or 15 different characters to cre- ate these 20,000 people. I also did a one-day motion capture shoot in Munich with one guy to capture walking, running, turning a corner and so on, and we plugged that into Massive. We did cloth simulations for all the people, as well, so their skirts and other clothing would move. You can tell if the clothes are not moving, even on a character that’s only a few pixels tall. It makes a big difference.”

Uncharted Territory 3D artist and lighting TD Robert Freitag wrote a somewhat auto- mated system for this and other snowy CG shots, which was employed in conjunction with custom, hand-painted work. “The snow system would calculate the angle of walls and building rooftops,” Weigert explained. “Up to a certain degree of steepness, a wall or rooftop would have snow on it; and at another angle, it wouldn’t. We could essentially press a button and this system would cover the rooftops and walls of the entire city with snow. We had to do displacement maps for closeups — for example, when the shot flies fairly close over the London Bridge on the big frozen Thames shot — but the snow in all of the wider shots was done using this automated system.”

The second shot to consume nearly a year of Uncharted Territory’s time on the show was the big aerial establishing view of the Tower of London. Due to the fact that most of the Tower had been rebuilt after the 17th century, the iconic structure

was a digital fabrication, as was the sprawling city around it. Photographs of the present-day Tower did provide some textures, but even those had to be modified somewhat for the sake of authenticity.

The intricate detail in the shot included hundreds of trees, each of which had hand -animated leaves and branches. “We built one master tree first,” said Weigert, “which was fully rigged. Then we created three versions of that master tree for summer, fall and winter, and an additional five different types of trees. So we had five different trees and three different versions of each, and by moving those around, we populated the shot with hundreds of trees. Fortunately, if you rotate a tree even ten degrees, it looks like a completely different tree.” The team initially experimented with a physics-based simulation to get some subtle movement of the leaves and branches, but was dissatis- fied with the results. “When the simulation proved disappointing, we hand-animated the five different trees. That movement made a big difference in making the shot look real. We also put cats on rooftops, added dogs, pigs, cows and horses walking around — all animated and full 3D. We had laundry hanging between buildings, too, which was all cloth simulated so it would move in the wind and give the shot some life.”

This and other wide establishing shots of the city also featured hundreds of smoke- stacks emitting smoke, which Uncharted Territory did in Digital Fusion, using a plug-in developed by compositing TD Robert Zeltsch, a Digital Fusion specialist. “We could just load the 3D model into the compositing software,” said Weigert,

“click on all the chim- neys to mark them, and this plug-in would automatically place our particle system smoke at those spots. We had several smoke setups, and they were all simulated — fully 3D, but in the compositing software. We did ten or fifteen different smoke simula- tions for variety. Some moved faster than others, some were heavier, and some were more wispy.”

A
ll of the wide CG shots benefited from what the team learned on the frozen Thames and Tower of London shots. One lesson learned was that lighting to suggest overcast London weather was a major obstacle in creating a photorealistic look. “It is so much easier to make a CG shot look real in full sunlight, with harsh shadows,” said Volker Engel. “In a more milky, overcast light, CG tends to look a little fake. So we had shots — such as the Tower of London shot — that started out more overcast, but wound up with more sunlight. We’d try several different lighting scenarios until we were happy with the look. One of the nice things about our long relationship with Roland is that he trusts us. So when we come to him, and say, ‘We’ve tried everything to make this shot work, and we need to change the lighting,’ he knows we’re serious about it.”

In addition to the lighting, camera angle was a key factor in whether a CG shot looked synthetic or real. “Roland wanted a lot of wide shots looking down onto the city,” said Marc Weigert,” but as we know from miniatures, that is the hardest thing to do. When you look down on something, even if it is full scale, it suddenly looks

like a miniature! In this case, the CG looked like a miniature in those shots. We fought that by putting more and more detail into the shots, including all of these little animated elements, which immediately told you it wasn’t a miniature.”

Another category of shots combined digital and greenscreen elements with background plates Weigert had captured during the initial pre-shoot period. Production wound up using those plates in only a dozen shots. One of them, a view of boats rowing up the Thames towards the palatial estate of William Cecil, was comprised of photography of a real mansion, augmented by the visual effects team to give it grander scale, plus 3D water and greenscreen boat elements.

Weigert and his small team had photographed the English mansion, intending to use it as it was for establishing shots, but Emmerich later decided that the mansion was not big enough to represent the Cecil estate. “We wound up having to expand on that quite a bit,” recalled Volker Engel. “We sent our visual effects art director, Greg Strasz, out to take more pictures, and then we did matte painting and projections to add big towers and that sort of thing to the original house.”

The Thames in the shot — as in all the shots featuring the river — was entirely 3D, generated through a new 3D water system developed by Eyeon. “They developed 3D water inside the compositing system,” said Marc Weigert, “and that’s something nobody has done before. Normally, when we do 3D in a package like Maya, for example, we have to set up the cameras, the animation, the textures, the shading and so on, and then we render it out and put that render in. So every time

we want to change something — like move the camera over — we have to go back into the 3D system, move the camera there, render the whole thing out again, and put it into the compositing system; and two days later we see what we actually did. On this, we tried to shift as much into the compositing realm as we could. Part of that was doing all of the water in compositing. So our senior compositor, Pieter van Houte, created a full 3D water setup — the river and the waves and everything — that could be handled in the compositing system, almost in real time. That allowed us to do a lot of iterations, very easily.”

Boats were either CG — textured with images of a real vessel of the period that had been photographed in Norway — or, more often, greenscreen elements. “We didn’t have a single real boat that we shot on the water,” said Weigert. “Roland was opposed to that because he knew from past movies how long it takes to shoot anything on water, and we only had about 55 shooting days on this movie. So I shot all the boat stuff as second-unit greenscreen. We built a modular boat made out of four pieces. We could make a very long boat if all the pieces were in; or we could take out a middle piece and make a shorter boat, and take yet another piece out to make an even smaller boat. We just wheeled it onto the stage, which had a greenscreen floor, and created a sense of rocking motion by putting it on air bags and having two people on either side rock it manually. Later, we added the interaction of the water on the boat, the wakes and so on, because none of that existed.” Reflections were automatically generated in the final composite. “We put

the 2D layer of the boat onto the water surface in the correct 3D space, and the 3D water automatically created appropriate reflections because the boat was in the correct position. It wasn’t 3D, but it looked correct because we were only seeing it from one specific angle.”

Another shot that utilized one of the pre-shoot plates is a view looking out the window of Cecil’s house, as the young Earl of Oxford (Jamie Campbell Bower) — his new ward — rides up a hedge-lined pathway. Production poured sand to create a short seg-ment of the path, and then shot the riders from a crane that was elevated to a height of three stories. The original plate had pools of sunlight on the path, which the crew replicated on its partial greenscreen set to ensure the riders would be moving in and out of light appropriately.

A
wintry scene in front of the Earl of Oxford’s house between the Earl and his wife (Paula Schramm) started with a plate of an actual house shot in England; but the effects team replaced the real structure with an all- synthetic one when the increase in the scale of the Cecil mansion suddenly made the Earl’s house look too small. “We completely scrapped the house that was in the original plate,” said Volker Engel, “and came up with an all new building for the Earl of Oxford’s house, which we made from scratch. We shot the actors with some real hedges and a real gate in the foreground, but those were the only real elements in the shot. Everything behind them is CG.”

To sell the illusion of bitter cold in the scene, Rony Soussan developed an efficient method for adding puffs of breath coming out of the mouths of the characters, applied together with compositing lead Ryan Smolarek. “Rony developed a system that could analyze the actual sound wave files,” explained Marc Weigert. “Wherever there were spikes in the sound files, the system would output these particles. The animators just had to make sure that little emitter was where the mouth was, and it would automatically spit out the breath as the character spoke. Breath coming out when they were not talking, we created by hand.” The system also automated the amount of breath ema-nating from the mouth. “We did a lot of analysis of past breath effects and noticed that the breath was often too thick. In real life, when the breath first comes out of the mouth, you hardly see it at all. It is only when it moves away from the mouth and starts to dissipate that you really see it. We developed a little thing to automate that, as well.”

T
he final 300 effects shots in Anonymous were executed to a level of sophistication that was made possible, in part, by the collaborative spirit between Roland Emmerich and his trusted Uncharted Territory colleagues, and by an efficient approach to effects work that Volker Engel and Marc Weigert have honed in their 23 years in the business. “Doing effects with our own in-house setup is a hell of a lot of work,” Weigert admitted, “and during every production, I always get to a point where I’m pulling out my hair, and thinking, ‘Why didn’t we just
go with an outside effects house?’ But it’s worth it because it gives us the ability to be right there on the floor, to walk in and sit next to an artist anytime we want. We can work with them throughout a shot’s development and see its progress every step of the way — instead of having to wait and see it every two weeks, and then saying, ‘Sorry, but you worked two weeks in the wrong direction.’”

Easy access to Roland Emmerich also made for efficient shot production. “I could go to the set with my laptop and show Roland an iteration of a shot, and get feedback immediately,” said Volker Engel. “So we had this incredibly fast turnaround on everything. In half an hour we could get an answer and do a new iteration. And then, in postproduction, we were right next to editorial. We’d all be on the same computer network, so we could go into the editing system and grab shots from them.

“We also built automated tools to help us react and do new iterations very quickly. As we were sitting in the screening room, for example, our notes and Roland’s notes would immediately show up on the artist’s desktop. So the artist could work on it, and re-submit the shot, and in that same session — literally, twenty minutes later — we would be able to see the new version of the shot. Then Roland could look at it, and say, ‘Okay, fine, approved.’ We could final a shot in twenty minutes, instead of waiting until the next day or the next week. We were very proud of that feedback loop, and it was really valuable. That was the kind of thing that made it worthwhile to do all of this work in-house.”
With Anonymous, a marked departure from his usual catastrophe-themed epics, director Roland Emmerich makes a plausible case that the plays attributed to William Shakespeare were actually written by the Earl of Oxford (Rhys Ifans).
Though the film required none of the visual effects spectacle that had characterized Emmerich’s past work, effects were nonetheless critical to creating the story’s iconic time and place. Emmerich relied on visual effects supervisors Marc Weigert and Volker Engel of Uncharted Territory — his filmmaking collaborators for more than two decades — to realize the production’s 300 effects shots, most involving computer generated depictions of late 16th- and early 17th-century England. For wide views of London, component-based structures were mixed and matched for variety, and arrayed on a period-accurate map grid of the city.
Relying on digital technology, Emmerich opted to build very little in the way of physical sets for Anonymous. Instead, the director shot the majority of scenes — even exteriors — on stage, with actors and minimal set pieces surrounded by greenscreen. Using photogrammetry techniques, Uncharted Territory filled out the spare greenscreen elements with fully 3D environments, often making use of plates and stills of existing 16th- and 17th-century structures that Marc Weigert photographed in villages throughout England prior to the start of principal photography.
