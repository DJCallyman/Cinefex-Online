By the start of Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows: Part Two — the eighth film produced by Heyday Films and Warner Bros. Pictures from J.K. Rowling’s books — things were looking bad for fledgling wizard Harry Potter. Harry (Daniel Radcliffe) and his fellow students, Her mione Granger (Emma Watson) and Ron Weasley (Rupert Grint), had so far retrieved only three of seven Hor cruxes — talismans containing the splin tered soul of Harry’s nemesis, the Dark Lord Vol demort (Ralph Fiennes) — and the trio had failed to prevent the evil wizard from gaining the all - powerful Elder Wand, which potentially gave its owner the ability to cheat death. With the fate of the wizard world on his slight shoulders, Harry is forced out of hiding to confront Voldemort and his Death Eater army at Hogwarts School of Witchcraft and Wizardry for a final all - out war. The precise structure for the final installment in Harry Potter’s cinematic saga remained closely guarded during the production of Part One while director David Yates and screenwriter Steve Kloves decided how best to main tain suspense between two films shot over 18 months, released six months apart. earlier, suggested a paradigm shift for visual effects. “We did a breakdown of Deathly Hallows while we were working on the sixth Potter film, in 2008,” recalled Tim Burke. “We didn’t have a script, but we realized that if we approached the battle of Hogwarts using our 1/24 - scale miniature, as we’d done for all the other films, we’d need hundreds of model shots, with multiple scale pieces at different sizes and multiple levels of destruction. It was looking like months of motion control shooting, which would have been a huge and daunting task.” Burke and Norton challenged Double Negative to create a fully digital Hog warts detailed enough to serve as the arena for the series’ grand finale. Double Negative lead model builder Pietro Ponti initiated digital construction with a survey of the 32 - foot - tall, 1/24 - scale Hogwarts miniature that Cinesite miniature effects supervisor José Granell had built and maintained at Shepperton Studios since 2003. “We photo graphed the model from a predetermined 360 - degree array of camera positions,” said Double Negative visual effects supervisor David Vickery. “We used flat lighting conditions, shooting with Canon 1Ds Mark IV cameras at as high a resolution as possible, capturing 5K stills of even the tiniest of turrets. We took that material back to Double Negative, along with lidar scans and our own Total Station surveys, and then started building.” Pro duc tion designer Stuart Craig, a Potter veteran since the first movie, provided a powerful resource. “We catalogued more than 1,300 architectural scale drawings from Stuart’s set designs. He had drawings of every tower, details of the way tiles on the roof overlapped, joints they used to fit one piece of timber into the next.
We had it all.” Plowman Craven & Associates also supplied lidar scans of new sets built for the picture, and Pietro Ponti oversaw surveys and photo studies of real - world structures that the production had used either as locations or as inspiration for Hogwarts’ design. “We had hundreds of thousands of high - resolution panoramic images,” said Vickery. “Pietro went to Durham, Glou cester and Salis bury Cathedrals, Edinburgh Castle and Dunkeld Cathe dral in Scotland. One of Hogwarts’ main buildings facing onto the courtyard is actually called the ‘Durham Build ing,’ because it was based on part of Durham Cathe dral. Stuart had created this fantastic structure based on real buildings, which gave it another level of fidelity.” Double Negative lead environment artist Kieron Hels don and previsualization super - visor Ferran Domenech then worked with Craig to consolidate their reference into a defin - itive Hogwarts layout. “There were huge changes to the school in Deathly Hal lows,” observed Vick ery. “For the battle sequence, the main part of the action took place in the courtyard, which previously was not very big. We extended that by two - thirds in length and breadth. Hogwarts’ stone viaduct never sat directly at the end of the courtyard, but we adjusted that to allow Voldemort a way to cut off all methods of escape. We also extended some of Hogwarts’ towers by three or four stories.” A major action set piece, conceived early in pre pro duction, involved the wooden bridge that extended out the rear of the castle toward a hillside — rallying point for a horde of Voldemort’s mercenary Snatchers. “David Yates wanted to blow up the wooden bridge like The Bridge on the River Kwai,” Tim Burke com mented. “That gave us an idea
of the scale he was thinking of, and we built our assets accord ingly. Ferran Domenech and his team prevized the scene while they were still animating sequences for Part One. We had to plan that far ahead because our shooting schedule was so complex. We didn’t finish shooting Part One until the summer of 2010, but we shot scenes for the end of Part Two in the summer of 2009, and climactic scenes with Ralph Fiennes in October 2009.” The complex schedule impacted practical effects with makeup effects supervisor Nick Dudman and special effects supervisor John Richardson, who continued their long associations with the production from their studios at Leavesden. Dudman’s team geared up for prosthetics and character makeups. Richardson’s team meanwhile prepared large-scale destruction effects through shooting. “Every set ended up being destroyed in one shape or form,” commented John Richardson. “In some circumstances we shot a set destroyed before we shot it intact; or we shot it intact before we shot it destroyed, and then we went back to intact, and then back to destroyed again!” The nonlinear schedule was the result of multiple intersecting storylines that crosscut between characters to heighten tension in the battle. Visual effects then linked scenes with dramatic swooping shots across Hogwarts’ terrain. “If we were on one side of the school with Voldemort and his army on the hill,” explained Tim Burke, “we would then fly down into Hogwarts and find teachers and students preparing defenses. Those kinds of linking scenes would have been impossible to do without virtual environments.” Pre - viz determined at what resolution each part of the school would have to be built. “After a few months of previz, we realized that we could get away with building 180 degrees
of the school; then we cherry - picked ‘hero’ areas and built those at higher resolution so we could transition in and out of sets.” The quantity and complexity of visual effects re quired a ski llful division of labor. “We decided to think of Dou ble Negative as our ‘miniatures’ and camera data vendor,” explained Burke, “letting them focus on one side of the castle and the attack on the wooden bridge. We then drew a virtual line across the front of Hog warts’ courtyard, and gave shots involving the viaduct and the environment beyond that, including the hillside where Voldemort’s army arrives, to MPC. They built the environment from that side of the school and generated the attacking army using their Alice crowd simulation software. Each vendor had its own models, but they shared assets.” To make shot sharing possible — with 13 visual effects vendors, and an additional seven companies involved in stereoscopic conversion — the previz team developed a low - resolution model of Hogwarts as a template for the environment. “MPC visual effects supervisor Greg Butler and I worked closely with Ferran Domenech’s team,” ob served David Vickery. “We built a proxy model of the school with all the correct con figurations. Kieron Helsdon then worked with Stuart Craig to define the surrounding mountain layout — which had changed from movie to movie. Kieron and Stuart put together a photo - montage of mountains. We made 3D models of those and created a Maya scene that we shared with all the facilities.” Every previous Potter film had conducted environment surveys of Hogwarts terrain in rural Scotland. Despite the wealth of scenic material already at hand, the diversity of
formats and lighting conditions in that foot age made it necessary for Double Negative to conduct a new location survey. “Pietro Ponti and Kieron Hels don put to gether a fantastic geometry reclamation shoot on hundreds of square kilometers of Scottish mountains,” said Vickery. “Pietro set up a three - camera rig of synchronized Canon 1Ds Mark IVs for a helicopter mount. He used Google Earth to plot semicircular flight patterns around each mountain. That made it possible to calculate exactly where the sun was going to be, to make sure lighting conditions would allow us to stitch the plates back together and relight them in postproduction. Pietro said it was the happiest day in his life when he turned up, and the pilot said: ‘Oh, you’ve got GPS. Just give that to me.’ The pilot used the data and hovered in all the positions we needed. Over a few weeks, they shot nearly half a terabyte of photographs.” Double Negative stitched images into panoramic vistas that could be projected onto 3D terrain. Artists detailed tex tures of grass and trees in Side Effects Houdini, ex - ported as RIB archives into Autodesk Maya, then painted textures to describe types and densities of vegetation, and used a particle system to scatter nodes that pro cedu rally generated trees. Double Negative conceptual artist Tania Richard then painted day and night sky domes that vendors shared and projected into scenes. Modeling techniques for Hogwarts structures were deter mined shot by shot, with detail focused on the court yard, Durham Building, Great Hall and battlements. Double Negative CG supervisor Rick Leary’s 30 - person 3D team used photographic projections in Maya to create high - resolution models of an intact version of the school three million polygons in size, and a version of the school in its destroyed state at a little under eight
million polygons. Higher - resolution details were sculpted in Mudbox and archived, enabling artists to sample displacements when rendering and compositing. “It was a very flexible system,” said Vickery. “We started with a high - resolution 3D mesh and added detail to that in Mudbox by referencing location photography. The result ing meshes were far too dense to be used interactively in Maya, so we baked all that detail back out to high - resolution displacement maps and applied those to more render - efficient geometry. More than half the work Pietro and his team did over two years was in creating detail. We placed dirt around windows, shrubs growing out the corners of window panes, and mortar lines between every brick. We also applied weath ering based on imaginary prevailing wind directions to make it look like certain structures had aged more than others.” The movie begins by revisiting the final scene of Deathly Hallows: Part One, with Voldemort seizing the Elder Wand, and then arrives at aerial views of Hog - warts surrounded by airborne dementors — robed specters in service to the Dark Lord. Rising Sun 2D digital effects supervisor Ed Hawkins added the dementors to Hog warts and its surroundings supplied by MPC and Double Negative.

The task of aging 19 - to 21- year - old actors into their late thirties was a daunting one for makeup effects. “We did it twice,” stated Nick Dudman. “The first time was on lo ca tion at Kings Cross. To prepare for that, Martin Rezard did a series of digital concepts, and makeup department head Amanda Knight and I discussed what we could achieve with color and makeup. We knew it had to look real; it could not jump off the screen as makeup. We did tests using tiny prosthetics. For the girls, we used little roll - on platgel appliances; but for them, it was more about hairstyles and costume. For the boys, we took their hairlines back fractionally with a forehead piece, gave them little jowls, eye bags, and tiny naso labial folds. We made body suits for all of them. The girls were fractionally thicker in the middle. The boys we made slightly stockier, but not so you’d really notice. It couldn’t be obvious what we’d done.” After the location shoot, Dudman reviewed footage with David Yates, and the filmmakers realized the illusion was not working. “David was very magnanimous,” said Dudman. “He wasn’t happy with the way he’d shot the scene; he felt he had tried to make too much of a statement, aging them too far. Voldemort appears to Harry in one of Hogwarts’ towers and extends his robes to ensnare the young wizard. The production initiated the effect of the robes capturing and raising Harry in midair by puppeteering Ralph Fiennes’ wardrobe and raising Daniel Radcliffe on wires. MPC generated tentacle-like cloth interactions of the Dark Lord’s robes unfurling and floating toward Harry. Nick Dudman’s team provided Voldemort makeup effects, which included complete facial coverage using silicone prosthetic appliances and tattoo transfer veining effects. Cinesite generated digital prosthetics to create Voldemort’s snake-like slitted muzzle.
Voldemort’s giant serpent, Nagini, menaces Ron and Hermione on Hogwarts’ main staircase. Cinesite generated digital set extensions and created dynamic rubble effects to enhance the set dressing and destroy sections of the staircase when hit by wand effects. MPC supplied Nagini, using the digital character it had developed for the previous film, and animated the creature’s death in a climactic scene in which mild-mannered Neville Longbottom (Matthew Lewis) heroically severs the serpent’s head, unleashing a burst of Horcrux energy.
The final confrontation between Voldemort and Harry takes place in Hogwarts’ devastated courtyard. David Yates filmed the sequence on a Leavesden backlot set, which production designer Stuart Craig conceived as significantly larger than previous Hogwarts courtyards. Double Negative supplied architectural extensions, layered in background terrain derived from Scottish scenic textures, and added a matte-painted sky. Cinesite provided Voldemort nose replacements, and MPC created wand effects — animated with a powerful new fluid simulation system — that preserved the color palette of wand blasts seen in previous Potter films.
The destruction of each Horcrux causes the erosion of the Dark Lord’s powers. Nick Dudman’s team manifested Voldemort’s deterioration with subtle bruising and broken skin effects in Ralph Fiennes’ makeup, which Cinesite incorporated into the digital nose prosthetic. As Harry gains the upper hand in the duel, forcing dark magical energy back toward Voldemort, Double Negative extended the Hogwarts terrain and MPC generated wand effects retracting into Voldemort’s wand.
For Voldemort’s death at the hands of the young Harry Potter, MPC tracked Fiennes’ performance and fashioned effects showing the Dark Lord’s eyes glazing over, and his flesh withering and flaking away.
Nineteen years later, the three wizard friends — now in their late thirties — meet at King’s Cross to send their children off on their first year at Hogwarts. After extensive testing of prosthetic makeup effects with Nick Dudman, and an earlier location shoot that revealed the aging effects as being too extreme, the production reshot the scene on a partial set at Leavesden. Dudman’s team refined the prosthetics, using ultra-fine silicone appliances and subtle body padding. Framestore created set extensions derived from photographic surveys of King’s Cross, and Lola Visual Effects further augmented age makeups with subtle digital effects.
