From its inception, Cap tain America: The First Avenger— Marvel Enter tain ment’s film based on the 1941 comic book by Joe Simon and Jack Kirby — was not geared to be a typical superhero film, in which the protagonist’s supernatural powers are illustrated with hundreds of splashy effects shots. “The 1,100 visual effects shots we were planning were designed mainly to anchor the film in the 1940s,” stated visual effects producer Mark Soper. “We knew we were going to have a lot of set extensions, matte paintings and 3D hard-body objects — cars, tanks, planes — spe cific to the era, but the feeling was that this was more like a war movie.” The script by Christopher Markus and Stephen McFeely introduced Steve Rogers (Chris Evans), an ear nest, but slight young man who dreams of enlisting in the U.S. Army at the outset of World War II. An Army scientist, Professor Abraham Erskine (Stanley Tucci), recruits Steve as a test subject in a classified project involving a serum that physically transforms him into a powerful warrior, who comes to be known as ‘Captain America.’ Steve is soon hurled into a conflict with Nazi General Johann Schmidt (Hugo Weaving), head of the Third Reich’s Deep Science ‘Hydra’ division, whose meg alomaniac goals are Soldier Blue BY JOE FORDHAM

To brainstorm an approach for creating the period adventure, visual effects super visor Christopher Townsend began planning the production with Mark Soper at Manhattan Beach Studios, in Southern California, where director Joe Johnston and production designer Rick Heinrichs set up a base of operations prior to principal photography at Shepperton Studios, in England. Johnston eschewed typical 3D previsualization — relegating animatics mostly to ‘post - viz’ generated at The Third Floor — and relied instead on designs and storyboarded concepts to initiate discussions with the main vendor, Double Negative. Concepts for Hydra technology began with lead vehicle designer Daniel Simon, who extrapolated on actual unrealized Nazi plans for military hardware that often resem bled science fiction. Special effects supervisor Paul Corbould constructed full - scale work ing ver sions of Schmidt’s 30 - foot - long Mercedes coupe, a one - man submarine and portions of other large vehicles mounted on gimbal rigs. Double Negative then used blue prints, set photography and lidar scans to build CG assets. “The military hardware was huge,” said Double Negative visual effects supervisor Charlie Noble. “There was a Landkreuzer tank based on actual Nazi plans for an armored vehicle about the size of a house. There was a Sub - Orbital Bomber, about 550 feet wide — approx i mately 50 per cent larger than any plane that has ever existed. In the Marvel world, Hydra has factories dotted around Europe to manufacture and assemble those vast machines, and we had to fill those environments with a lot of detail.”
In addition to Double Negative’s contribution, the film’s visual effects assignment involved a complex interaction between prosthetic effects overseen by make up effects designer David White and digital effects created at Framestore and Lola Visual Effects for Johann Schmidt’s disfigurement as the hideous ‘Red Skull.’ Lola also handled a key illusion, featured in the first third of the film, which enabled Chris Evans to portray Steve Rogers’ scenes both as ‘Skinny Steve’ and as his buffer, taller alter ego. “Joe Johnston wanted Chris to portray the character throughout,” stated Christopher Townsend. “We talked about ways to do that, doing full digital head replace - ment — like in Benjamin Button— putting Chris’ head onto a skinny actor, and doing three - dimensional projections of Chris’ face and body onto three - dimensional geometry, which we would then scale down to Skinny Steve. We also talked about a 2D approach, manipulating Chris’ image frame by frame. The most important thing to Joe was that the essence of Chris’ performance be retained.” To explore options for digitally manipulating a performer’s size and height, the production shot a film test of two actors — one slender, one buff — sitting, walking, shaking hands and otherwise interacting together within a minimal office set. After putting the actors through the exercise both fully clothed and bare - chested, production challenged vendors to slim the muscular actor to the skinny performer’s size. Lola adapted an approach it had developed to create digi tal reverse - aging illusions for productions such as X - Men 3 and The Curious Case of Benjamin Button. “They wanted to keep the big guy’s body in the frame,” explained Lola senior artist Casey Allen. “So we took a plate of the muscular actor and, through a series of deforms and
distortions specific to certain areas of his body, we shrank him down and retained his performance.” The deformations created the impression of reduced muscle mass scaled appropriately to the performer’s skeletal structure. A paint - and - patch texture transferal technique helped preserve natural skin textures. “We’ve developed a lot of techniques over the years to deal with that. If we lose texture in an area, we can lift a texture off of the original section and then track that back in.” The test footage won Lola the assignment to generate Skinny Steve effects, and informed techniques used during principal photography. “We found that shrinking someone from six - foot - five to shorter in the same screen space made him look like he was sliding as he walked,” said Lola visual effects supervisor Ed son Williams. “If we had a four - foot stride and shrank that to a three - foot stride, he lost a foot in every step. It helped if Chris’ feet landed where they were supposed to in the real footage; so they made Chris Evans aware of that, and asked him to take shorter steps. They also sometimes asked him to do what we called the ‘Groucho Marx’ — he would walk into a room and shrink down six inches by crouching at his knees. If he did that on wider shots, we would straighten his legs digitally to make it look like he was walking upright. That way, we didn’t have to replace the background ob scured behind his head. It was particularly useful if there was a crowd behind him.” The production used no tracking markers — except at times on Leander Deeny, a 125 - pound English theater actor who served as Evans’ Skinny Steve body double — and made sparing use of 3D data gathering. “At one point, we felt there was a chance that
we would have to go a full- CG route,” remarked Christopher Townsend. “We rolled wit- ness cameras to cover ourselves; but to Lola’s credit, they made it work without that, and so we didn’t need a lot of that material. We didn’t know that going in, though. While we were shooting, the script supervisor would often ask, ‘What approach are you going to use?’ And I would have to say, ‘I don’t know!’ Joe would shoot a minute - and - a - half scene with one camera mov ing all over the place, so we often couldn’t use just one tech - nique for Skinny Steve. For that reason, we shot mul tiple passes of every Skinny Steve scene, just to cover ourselves.” For every Skinny Steve scene, Chris Evans wore oversized wardrobe to help create a sense of the character engulfed inside his clothes. After Evans established each performance, Leander Deeny repeated the take wearing a like ness makeup. “Prosthetic artist Jon Moore applied more than 20 prosthetic makeups on Leander,” stated makeup effects designer David White. “It was a seven - piece gelatin makeup designed to pull Leander a little closer to Chris. We enhanced his cheekbones, the bridge of his nose, his chin and bottom lip, and added little pieces on either side of his nostrils.” Deeny replicated Evans’ performance with remarkable accuracy. “Leander was an amazing mimic,” said Christopher Townsend. “He’d watch Chris perform, and then do the scene himself; and then I’d watch a 50/50 video mix of Leander and Chris’ playback, and we usually got what we needed in one take.” Background performers also repeated their actions while the grip and camera teams did their best to match the
master take. “It wasn’t motion controlled, so nothing was exact. We always shot a clean background plate for each Skinny Steve scene, so at least Lola would have something to help them rebuild backgrounds.” Lola rotoscoped or keyed Evans from the background plates and re - sculpted his physique using Autodesk Maya for 3D, Pixel Farm PFTrack and 2d3 boujou for 3D tracking and compositing in Autodesk Flame. The most widely used technique, known as ‘Steve slimming,’ relied on mesh warps and deformations to reduce Evans’ height and body mass by an average of ten percent. Evans’ powerful trapezius muscles, on either side of his neck, presented a particular challenge for the effects artists. Lola reduced muscle mass in the area by lightening shadows in the back of the actor’s jaw and on both sides of his neck before applying a concave warp. To slenderize Steve’s arms, Lola lightened shadows at the base of the biceps, which reduced muscular relief prior to the mesh warp. To reduce the size of Evans’ hands, artists generated individual mesh warps for each finger. Evans’ face was the most challenging area to slenderize. “We first tried making the head smaller with a rounder chin,” said Edson Williams. “That made him look too feminine, so we flattened the jaw - line a little, elongated the cleft from his nose to upper lip and reduced the distance between his bottom lip to his chin. We maintained his original nose and eyes, and brought up the rest of the face to meet that.” Facial deformations required pixel - accurate detail. “We had so many mesh - warps tugging all the different dis tortions, sometimes things got a little bit out of alignment. A
couple of times we brought the corners of the mouth down a little too much, and the production asked us, ‘Why is he frowning?’ When we examined the before and after, we realized that by lowering the corners of his mouth three pixels, we had altered his expression. We had to be very careful to make sure we didn’t turn a grimace into a smile, or a look of confusion into a look of anger. The hardest part was trying to maintain his orig inal intent.” In addition to Steve slimming, Lola used Leander Deeny passes to add skinny body parts to Evans’ body. “We created a few head replacements by putting Chris’ head on Leander’s body,” said Casey Allen. “We sometimes did the blend at the jaw - line, sometimes at the neck. It was tricky to make that work because Chris had a very distinctive Adam’s apple. Whenever he delivered his lines, his Adam’s apple moved. We had to be very careful to preserve that anatomy.” If neither slimming effects nor body part replacements were possible, Lola resorted to digital face projection. During postproduction, Chris Evans participated in a two - hour scanning session at Lola, re - enacting his facial performances for specific scenes. Artists then projected imagery of Evans’ face onto 3D geometry blended onto Deeny using a technique that Lola developed for twinning-effect face replacements in director David Fincher’s The Social Network. “The face replacement area was like a hockey player’s mask,” remarked Edson Williams. “We blended just below the hairline, down along the chin line and up to the ears. Leander’s ears were different enough from Chris’ that we had to modify them to match. We then made a seam along the ear, just under the jaw. We only used that technique if Chris’ performance was so dissimilar to Leander’s there
was no way to align the two. That determination was dic tated by production. The film opens in an Arctic blizzard, where present - day scientists discover a giant gull - winged aircraft half - buried in the ice. The production filmed the scene at Shep perton using a practical snowcat and a portion of the aircraft. Fuel VFX digitally generated additional snowcats and enhanced an in - camera effect of the Arctic sun, which cinematographer Shelly John son achieved by bouncing a light source against a white wall, creating a pale disc of light through swirling snow and fog. Fuel removed the soundstage ceiling, enhanced the horizon, placed additional scientists around the frozen aircraft, then animated effects of a laser slicing an access port through the ice. The action then switches to Tønsberg, Norway, 1942, where Nazi stormtroopers arrive in search of an artifact hidden in a crypt in a village church. The filmmakers shot village night exteriors on location in Culross, Scot land. Double Negative enhanced the en vironment with a digital matte painting and a digital Landkreuzer tank, which caves in a crypt wall. Paul Corbould’s team staged the wall collapse on a soundstage set using a tank - shaped form and breakaway effects. Double Negative 3D lead Phil Johnston and compositing lead Scott Pritchard added dynamic simulations of crumbling masonry. Johann Schmidt enters the crypt and discovers a secret hiding place from which he retrieves a cube of glowing energy, the ‘Tesseract.’ Property master Barry Gibbs created a practical prop for Weaving to wield, illuminated via an array of 50 ultra - bright light emit ting diodes. Double Negative effects leads Gavin Graham and Nicola Hoyle enhanced
the Tesseract glow. “The brief was to make the cube energy not too electrical,” said Double Negative 2D supervisor Andy Lockley. “It had to look menacing and dangerous, but also have a cosmic feel to it. We put some shots together with generic elements in 2D, but for the most part we art-directed animation curves in Side Effects Houdini, along which crackles of energy traveled.” Schmidt departs the ruined crypt in his Mercedes coupe, a monstrous limousine that Paul Corbould’s team built from conceptual art by Daniel Simon. Double Negative developed a CG model of the Mercedes to generate interactive reflections of CG objects in the vehicle’s glossy surface. “The practical Mercedes had so many layers of paint,” said Charlie Noble, “we had to write special shaders to create the feeling of very thick layers of lacquer, which from certain angles reflected purple underneath. As the tank pulled away from the crypt, both vehicles were completely CG.” Schmidt later experiments on the Tesseract cube in a secluded mountaintop retreat overlooking an Alpine valley. Second - unit visual effects supervisor Stephane Ceretti shot aerial plates for Schmidt’s mountaintop laboratory in Sion, Switzerland. Double Nega tive 3D lead Dan Neal and compositing lead Astrid Busser - Casas then assembled the envi - ron ment using additional textures shot in England. “The original plan was to shoot as many plates as possible,” said Double Negative CG supervisor Peter Bebb. “During post - production, the edit evolved and camera angles changed, so we had to build a fairly ex - tensive Alpine environment in 3D.” Meanwhile, in New York City, Steve Rogers undergoes a U.S. Army recruitment medical examination. out and the wire falling. Lola then sampled pieces. “We rotoscoped Chris Evans from the plate,” related Edson Williams. “The Leander plate was too dissimilar to other takes, so we discarded that. We did all our slimming effects on Chris, and combined that with the clean plate. The deceptively simple scene involved some of the most technically demanding slimming effects. Johnston filmed Tucci entering the cabin, the camera pulling back and panning past a bunk where Evans sat. The camera move, with parallax shifts through the room, made it impossible to match repeated
background passes to the master plate. Instead, Lola rotoscoped Evans from frame and digitally rebuilt the room. “We spent approximately 500 man - hours on that shot,” said Williams, “with 420 of those hours spent rebuilding the background. We had to rebuild the entire room in 3D, then project textures from the clean plate onto that geometry, and realign that to the master plate, which gave us our clean background.” Layering Skinny Steve back into the scene required fine detail compositing where greenscreen separations were not possible. “Greenscreens were most useful in keying separations on the hair,” noted Williams. “If we handled that with rotoscoping, we’d often get notes that the hair looked ‘compy’ because we couldn’t isolate such small hairs. We tried using luminance keys, but that was not possible if there was a bright background. For a lot of the hair mattes with Steve slimming, we ended up doing matte paintings of a dozen hairs. We tracked those to the head in 3D, which broke up the matte line with a dozen flyaway hairs and helped sell the illusion.” Evans’ costume for the barracks scene — an undershirt with dogtags hanging in front of his chest — also presented a challenge as mesh - warps covering Evans’ body caused rigid objects to deform, turning buttons oval and causing dogtags to shrink and enlarge. To prevent the anomalies, artists either removed and recomposited elements, or adjusted meshes to preserve rigid objects’ natural interactions. Moments where Steve made contact with rigid objects — reaching into his suitcase to pick up books, or accepting a glass of liquor from Erskine — also required complex warps, reducing the scale of Evans’ hand as it reached through frame, then warping the hand into correct proportion with the object as it made contact.

David White created a sculpture based on Hugo, and we used that as our base. We discovered that because Hugo has a very strong jaw, we needed to make him appear gaunter. So we experimented digitally, squaring up the jaw, thinning the lower lip, and changing the size of Hugo’s eyes, trying to find the perfect look that we could then enhance.” Prosthetic concepts explored degrees of Red Skull’s manic intensity. “Our initial
sculp ture was very visceral and stylized,” said David White. “It had a lot of muscle structure and veins. Joe immediately picked up on that and asked us to make it look cleaner, like skin vacuformed over a skull. I came up with some ideas to incor porate Hugo’s char acter, stripped away the muscle and kept it really lean with classic shapes.” White’s team experimented with practical effects to distort Weaving’s face, including a spring - loaded dental appliance that fit inside the actor’s mouth to pull his lips into a permanent snarl. The production ultimately limited the makeup to a seven - piece pros thetic, engineered by mold designer and silicone specialist Dan Meeker, covering Weaving’s head and neck with appliances that allowed a full range of expression. “We created pieces out of encapsulated silicone,” explained White. “We sprayed cap plastic onto the surface of the mold, pieced the mold together, and injected silicone pigmented to create an appropriate base color. We then pre - painted pieces using PAX paints, and did tests with translucency. If the material was too dense, it appeared dead. If it was too translucent, it turned into jelly and lost translucency as soon as we applied any color. It took a lot of tests to get the right balance.” Prosthetic artist Shaune Harrison and prosthetic assis tant Victoria Holt assisted White with Red Skull applications, which progressed from the back of the head, across the cranium, face, neck and ears, with a separate nose bridge, which artists painted black. Deep red eyeliner took Red Skull’s crimson skin texture to the actor’s eyeballs. A dental prosthetic completed the makeup, giving Weaving’s bottom lip a Brando - esque pout. In addition to silicone prosthetics, foam latex specialist Con McCloskey generated 30 slip - on masks for Red Skull stunt performers.

Captain America re-boards the bomber and has a final showdown with Red Skull on the aircraft’s flight deck, where the Tesseract cube becomes dislodged from its central energy core. Joe Johnston staged the flight deck fight on a gimbal set that simulated banking and diving motions of the bomber as it lost control. David White’s team supplied Red Skull makeup effects, which Lola Visual Effects supplemented with nose removals and subtle facial alterations. Double Negative generated window views and created cube energy effects, enhancing the illuminated prop that Hugo Weaving wielded on set.
During the knockdown fight, the bomber’s central power cube, clasped by Schmidt, causes the crazed megalomaniac to be sucked out of the aircraft in a beam of energy. The production filmed the scene using bright interactive light on Hugo Weaving, who held the illuminated cube prop. Double Negative then generated crackling cube energy and a digital double of Red Skull, which eroded and streamed up through the cockpit roof toward another dimension.
A shock wave emanates from the Sub-Orbital Bomber as Schmidt exits on the energy beam. Steve takes the controls, struggling in vain to divert the bomb-laden craft from its preset American targets, and ultimately crashes into the Arctic where he remains frozen in suspended animation until the icebound wreckage is discovered in modern times and he is thawed out to join the Avengers.
