For director Jon Favreau, a mild curiosity about the intriguingly titled Cowboys & Aliens— a film project based on a 2006 graphic novel, which was under development at DreamWorks — grew into real interest after a chance encounter with screenwriters Roberto Orci and Alex Kurtzman at 2009’s Comic-Con, where he was pro moting the 2010 release of Iron Man 2. “I’d thought that the title was compelling,” Fav reau recalled, “but I didn’t know much about it. And then I ran into Bob and Alex, who were just fin ish ing the script, and they sent it to me to read — and I connected with it immediately. That is rare. Usually you read a script and you can see the potential of it; but, in this case, it was all there. The tone was just right.” The screenplay related the story of an alien invasion of a small western town — Absolution, Arizona — in the year 1873, and the classic western - style counterattack against the invaders by a small band of cowboys, led by reformed gunslinger Jake Lonergan (Daniel Craig) and powerful rancher Woodrow Dolarhyde (Harrison Ford). It was Invasion of the Body Snatchers meets The Searchers…War of the Worlds meets The Magnificent Seven. Invasion of the Body Snatchers BY JODY DUNCAN
“I knew from the outset that this movie would have to make sense as both genres,” stated Favreau. “We couldn’t say, ‘We’re making a sci - fi movie, so we don’t have to worry if it doesn’t work within the logic of a western.’ The trick was to respect both genres by playing it straight — not satirizing one or the other or both. The original script played it straight, and that would have to continue with the performances, the casting, the effects, the music, and the editorial style. It was squarely on my shoulders, as the director, to nudge each of those elements one way or the other so that the film maintained the right tone.” While still in postproduction on Iron Man 2, Fav reau committed to the project, and began meet ings with Cowboys & Aliens’ slate of heavy - weight producers, which, in addition to Dream Works’ Steven Spiel - berg, included Brian Grazer and Ron Howard of Imagine Entertainment. The first order of business was to design the alien. Legacy Effects, with whom Favreau had worked on his previous three films, would build the practical version for use on set; but supervisor Shane Mahan made an impassioned case for letting his creature team design the alien, as well. “My pitch to the studio,” said Mahan, “was: ‘This is what we specialize in. Crea - tures — that’s what we do.’ There have been shows in the past where we’ve inherited designs, where they just hand you something, and say, ‘Here’s the alien.’ That’s fine; but in this case, given our history with the people involved, I said: ‘Let our team take a crack at this. You keep referencing films like Predator and Aliens, and those are films that we did with Stan Winston! So why not let us design the alien? If you don’t like it,
you can take over.’ They welcomed that approach.” Awarded the assignment, Legacy artists — led by Scott Patton and Ian Joyner, with Simon Webber playing a key role — started by creating a range of alien designs in Photo - shop and 3D modeling packages. “In a week,” recalled Scott Patton, “five of us came up with 67 different concepts.” “Designs ranged from elegant to insectoid to wormlike,” added Ian Joyner. “Some were multi - legged, some were two - legged, and some had no legs at all. One of the under - lying ideas was that it would be a kind of organic Transformer, with limbs that would emerge from other parts of the body. That Transformer idea was the basis for the little arms that come out of the chest, which was in the final design.” Jon Favreau had suggested that the alien have some characteristics of a sea turtle; and so the designers incorporated hard shell - like areas atop an underlying translucent skin in a late - stage digital sculpt. That digital model also had features meant to ensure that the alien would not look like a man in a suit — very narrow hips, and arms that were not only longer than those of a human, but also had hollow areas with bladders that ‘breathed.’ The creature’s height of eight feet would also work against its looking like a man in a suit, and was large enough to appear threatening in relation to characters on horses. “We wanted to make sure that the aliens weren’t dwarfed by people on horseback,” noted Favreau. “I wanted them to be a real physical threat, like the aliens in pre - CG films like Alien, Aliens and Predator.” Using the final digital sculpture, Legacy ‘grew’ a rapid prototype maquette in its 3D printer. “We painted that,” Shane Mahan commented, “and presented it to the producers
as a scaled model. It had separate heads showing different expressions, as well as the opened chest section. It really helped to give them an idea of exactly what this alien was going to be.” With the digital design approved, Legacy produced a full - scale version in foam. “We did some cleaning up on that,” said Mahan, “and then did a mold and produced a clay version so that we could detail the surface even more. We made traditional molds and ran skins to make it as light and yet durable as possible. It was a combination of silicone joints and urethane shells, and also foam rubber in some areas and fiberglass in others, with a central mechanical frame that everything pinned to. It also had interchangeable heads that we grew in our 3D printer.” Arms were rod - puppeted, and the body was on a manually driven motion gimbal and cart. Legacy also equipped the puppet with eyes that could bug out from the alien’s face, and a chest piece that irised open, and from which emerged smaller arms and hands. “Those little arms were rod puppets, as well, with mechanical fingers.” It would take eight to nine Legacy puppeteers — Chris Grossnickle, Johnnie Spence, Hiroshi Ikeuchi, Marc Free man, Cory Czekaj, Jamie Grove, Mark Killingsworth, Jim Kundig and Christopher Swift — to fully operate the full - scale puppet on set. “There was one person on each function,” Mahan noted. “One each for the head, neck, mouth, arms, chest, fingers, breathing and the body rig. And then, if a foot came in, we had an additional operator on that.” Prior to joining the production, Mahan organized a weekend shoot at Legacy’s studio to commit to video the range of performance that could be obtained from the two hero puppets. “We shot as much of the storyboards as possible
to educate Jon and crew as to what they could expect to get from the character on set. Often you don’t have time to do that sort of thing; but in this case, we had beaten the clock, and so we had time to rehearse and do this video. The test we shot was really ex - hilarating and fun. My daughter Caro line was even there, turning the lights on and off to make ‘lightning’ for us. We edited that together and showed it to about 30 production people. It was a real confidence builder for them.” While Legacy was building the practical alien, Industrial Light & Magic visual effects super visor Roger Guyett, who served as the film’s over - all visual effects supervisor, and ILM animation supervisor Marc Chu were overseeing animation tests to determine how the computer animated alien would look in ambulatory form against desert landscapes. “We scanned Legacy’s ma quette and rigged it to give us a digital version of the alien that we could move around inside the computer,” Guyett said. “We mocked up a simple environment made up of rocks and some desert terrain, and had the alien moving in that environment. It was pretty crude, but it captured the essence of the alien and what it was going to look like in the shots.” Guyett and Chu also choreographed combat vignettes to illustrate what the aliens would look like in confron tation with cowboy characters mounted on horses. “We animated some cowboys and horses and came up with little action scenarios,” said Marc Chu. “We had an alien jumping off a cliff to attack a cowboy on a horse. We had cowboys riding through a canyon, getting am bushed by aliens. By doing these little stories,
we started to learn the physical language of the alien.” Another purpose of the tests was to determine if the animation should be driven by motion capture or key framed. “The alien is more or less humanoid,” observed Chu, “so we did some motion capture tests very early on, just to see if we could get some of the movement that way. I took some of our animators down to our motion capture stage and captured some walk and run cycles and general motion — jumping over things, attacks, leaps, things like that — and those tests revealed that motion capture wasn’t the way to go. Once we put a human motion on that rig, it just ended up looking like a human. So we determined that this creature should be keyframed.” The animation tests also revealed a need for minor alterations to the Legacy design. “There are always problems with a design that you can’t know about until you actually start to move these creatures,” Chu stated. “For example, the initial design had a very narrow foot with a singular, pointed claw. But we found, once we started animating it, that we couldn’t get a sense of the alien having good purchase with that type of foot. So at ILM, our art and model departments did some work to redesign that foot and parts of the leg.” Presented to Favreau and the producers, the animated vignettes helped to earn the film its official greenlight. With that — and with the completion of Iron Man 2— Favreau turned his full attention to Cowboys & Aliens, meeting with department heads such as Roger Guyett, production designer Scott Chambliss and director of photography Matthew Libatique. Favreau also dove into storyboarding and previzing key sequences with Dan Gregoire of Halon Entertainment.
One issue that had to be settled early in preproduction was whether or not Cow boys & Aliens would be presented in 3D. “There was a huge push toward 3D around that time,” noted Roger Guyett, “partly because Avatar had just come out. So we felt we should at least try the 3D route and decide if it was appropriate for the movie. We did a bunch of tests with it, photographing people riding horses, the camera on a crane moving amongst them. It gave even that simple foot - age an interesting quality. I’m not saying it was better or worse — but it was definitely different.” “At the time,” elaborated Jon Favreau, “I was enthusiastic about exploring 3D. It was a new thing, and it seemed irresponsible not to learn everything I could about it before making a decision. But I didn’t want to do a conversion. I said, ‘Either we’re going to shoot it natively in 3D, or we’re just going to keep it 2D.’ The problem was that it wasn’t just 3D versus 2D, it was also video versus film. I thought it was important that we hold fast to the things that married the western and sci - fi genres — and one of the things that unified those two genres was the use of anamorphic lensing. Whether it is the lens flares in Close Encounters or showing the western landscape in a western, anamorphic lenses have brought something to those genres. So I was very excited about working in that format, as well, and so was Matty Libatique. Anamorphic would give the movie a classic feel that would be true to both genres.” In late May 2009, production set up in Santa Fe, New Mexico, about an hour or more from the scenic desert locations at which the crew would be shooting until Sep tember. Locations included Abiquiu, an area famously put to canvas by artist Georgia O’Keefe.
Some interior sets — such as the cabin where Jake had previously lived with his love, Alice (Abigail Spencer) — were built on stage at a studio in Santa Fe, although the major - ity of the studio work was done at Universal Studios, at the tail end of production. Cowboys & Aliens begins with Jake awakening in the desert — wounded, disoriented, his memory gone and a strange metallic band inexplicably affixed to his wrist. After fighting off bounty hunters, Jake steals one of their horses and makes his way to nearby Absolution. Production shot Absolution exteriors at Bonan - za Creek Ranch, originally a forced - perspective western town set laid out with smaller - scale buildings at one end of a main street and full - scale buildings at the other end. Scott Chambliss and his construction crews rebuilt much of the town, removing and rebuilding the forced - perspective elements, adding new facades to many of the existing buildings and erecting additional structures. The exterior town set served for some interior scenes, as well, such as those inside the saloon and the sheriff’s office. ILM enhanced a plate of the location for the initial reveal of the town in the distance, from Jake’s POV. “Early on,” said ILM visual effects co - supervisor Eddie Pas - quarello, “we did a version of the shot where we added a lot of enhancements to the town; but event u ally we went back to the original plate and painted out the modern - day stuff and added subtle matte - painting enhancements to give it more life.” It does not take long for Jake to find trouble in Abso lu tion, primarily with Percy Dolarhyde (Paul Dano), Wood row Dolarhyde’s bullying son. Dolarhyde’s ranch is the setting of the first alien attack in the film, when a chaotic flurry of bright lights and
weapons fire from above leaves shrubbery and unfortunate bovines set aflame, perplexing the drunken ranch hands who have camped near a stream. The sequence begins with a flash of light that sends one of the hands falling backward into the stream. “All of this mayhem is happening above him,” remarked Eddie Pasquarello, “but we’re not quite sure what’s going on. The audience is going through what the characters are going through, and so the attack is purposely very confusing and vague. And, of course, it is a total mystery to these characters be cause they’ve never even seen an aircraft, let alone an alien aircraft.” ILM augmented extremely bright rock - concert - style lighting used on location to simulate the attack. “The practical light wasn’t enough,” said Pasquarello, “so we added digital lighting effects. There’s also all this dust that is kicked up, which we enhanced with particle effects and comped smoke elements. With all the additional lighting, particle effects and smoke, we were able to make it look like one continuous blast that was much bigger than what we got on the shoot.” ILM composited in brief impressionistic flashes of aircraft flying overhead, as seen from the ranch hand’s POV under the water. The character then emerges from the stream, only to find large - scale devastation all around him. “The aftermath of the attack was an elaborate matte painting and composite with CG fire and burning cows. That was all done in digimatte. With the exception of the foreground characters, we replaced everything that was in the plate.” Environmental and safety regulations limited the scale and number of practical pyrotechnics that could be shot on location, and so ILM created most of the fire effects for this and other alien attack sequences using its proprietary Plume software. “Our
Plume tool is so good,” said ILM digital production supervisor Michael DiComo, “we went right to it for fire that required any kind of interactivity. Plume allows us to control the scale, the speed, everything. We have a massive stock library of great fire elements we’ve shot in the past; but to have total control, we use Plume.” For a subsequent sequence in which the aliens attack the town, special effects super visor Dan Sudick and his team were able to orchestrate a sig nificant number of large - scale explosions on the set; but the town attack still required dozens of visual effects shots, and was one of four centerpiece action and effects sequences in the film. The town attack begins shortly after Jake has been turned over to federal officers, charged with arson and the murder of Alice, whom we later learn has been abducted by the aliens. Sitting inside a jail wagon, Jake only gets brief glimpses of what is transpiring outside as alien aircraft — dubbed ‘speeders’ by production — fly in and discharge their light - emitting weapons, ravaging the town. The craft also shoot out long bolo - type tethers, with grappling hooks at the ends, which wrap around victims and hoist them into the sky. While Dan Sudick’s team orchestrated practical explo sions and massive laser lighting setups on location for the town attack, stunt coordinator Tom Harper had provided wire rigs to lift actors and stunt people into the air for the alien abductions. “For the most part,” said Roger Guyett, “we were able to capture those abductions on location, using a counter - balance type of rig that pulled the actors or stunt people off the ground and up to a height of about 30 to 40 feet. ”
Though the majority of speeder shots in the town attack and throughout the film would be executed by The Embassy, ILM took the creative lead on the speeder, modeling the digital asset to match art department de signs and practical versions built by Legacy Effects. “The speeder was a curious beast,” commented ILM CG supervisor Robert Weaver, who was instrumental in the look development of the alien craft. “It had five wings, which had lights on them and could spread like a dragon fly’s wings, and it had an arching-back capability. Although it was an aircraft, it looked and acted something like a creature; and so, when the audience and the characters first see it, they don’t know that there’s an alien piloting it. For all anyone knows, the speeder is the alien.” Like all the alien hardware, the speeder had been designed to look like something made by a technologically advanced culture, but with properties that would easily blend into 19th - century southwest environments. Stainless steel or slick, titanium - type finishes were rejected in favor of a more industrial - age look. “We made a shader for the speeder based off anisotropic materials,” Weaver explained, “so it had a metallic look, but with direction and depth, which we created through fine scratches and brushed - aluminum - type details. We added specularity to it, but it didn’t have an overall bright sheen, which would have looked out of place in the desert.” In addition to modeling the speeder, ILM animated key shots — led by animator Andy Wong — and lit and rendered them to give The Embassy a target to shoot for. As fantastic a craft as it was, Jon Favreau wanted the speeder’s flight animation to be based on real - world reference. “We referenced jet maneuvers such as banking to make the speeder animation look like it was grounded in a physical reality,” explained Marc Chu,
“and then, beyond that, we added a level of character to it, to make it clear that this wasn’t just a normal plane. It was quite an exploration to figure out how to make the speeder feel like an organic flying machine. For example, we had to determine what it would do when it was about to attack. We came up with this curling motion it would do just before it shot out a bolo and grabbed somebody down below. We worked closely with Jon to come up with that language; and then we passed that along to Embassy.” The Embassy delivered 70 - 80 speeder shots, each of which required its team to reshade and relight the ILM asset to accommodate the company’s XSI — rather than Maya - based — pipeline. “All the textures were there,” said Embassy visual effects supervisor Winston Hel gason, “but we had to reshade the speeder to make ours match to ILM’s. We also did some work to redesign the bolo. I think production was worried that the original bolos looked too much like stunt wires that had not been painted out properly. So we added a light system running along the length of the bolo, and put a lens - flare effect on the hooks to give them a more interesting look.” Embassy artists also did digital double work for the abductees, augmenting practical wirework that had been done on set by Tom Harper and his crew. “That was pretty complex character animation because we had to match to the stunt double’s work, and everything had to line up and track perfectly.” A key Embassy shot in the town attack sequence is set inside the jail wagon. As the attack progresses outside, Jake’s wristband — called the blaster — suddenly opens, projecting a holographic heads - up display that artists at ILM designed. “It was hard to come up with a new take on the heads - up display,” recalled Eddie Pas quarello. “It was
a high - tech, alien weapon, but it had to be something that would make sense to Jake in the 1870s, and also make sense to the audience. We came up with a fairly simple design for the hologram, based on a gun site, because that was something Jake would understand. Jon was very clear that everything had to make some sense to the characters of this time and place. For example, the bolo that grabs people was some thing that they would understand because it was similar to a lariat, and yet it suggested a sophisticated technology. There always had to be that marriage of 1870s reality and future alien tech.” Production had shot the jail wagon scene with Daniel Craig wearing a prop blaster in an opened position, and with interactive light from the heads - up display shining on his arm. “To actually show the blaster closed and then opening,” said Winston Helgason, “we had to re place his arm from the elbow down with a digital arm and blaster, complete with interactive lighting effects. The lighting was complicated because there were explo sions and strobe lights from the lasers outside shining on his arm. About 200 frames into the shot, we did a transition back to his real arm wearing the practical open blaster.” Now aware that his wristband is a lethal weapon, Jake aims it at a speeder and brings it down in a crash. On location, Roger Guyett rolled cameras as Dan Sudick’s team pulled a speeder - shaped mandrel through dirt to create a 30 - yard - long trench representing the craft’s path as it plows through the landscape. At the end of the trench was a practical crashed speeder fashioned by Legacy Effects, with flashing lights and
smoke effects. “Our job,” related Eddie Pas quarello, “was to animate the CG speeder coming in, and line up the crash to the on - set trench. Making sure that we matched into the prac tical element at the end made those composites tricky.” To pump up the shots, ILM added digital dust kick ing up behind the crashing speeder, as well as a dig ital heat trail. Jake and other townspeople cautiously approach the crashed speeder, and discover strange alien footprints tracking away from it, alerting them to the fact that ‘something’ is out there. The next day, Jake, Dolarhyde, Ella (Olivia Wilde) — a stranger in town who claims that her people, too, have been abducted by the aliens — and other towns - people form a posse to find and destroy the alien invaders. A stop at the log cabin he once shared with Alice stirs flashes of memory in Jake. Matty Libatique achieved the look of this and other flashback sequences via reverse - process, shooting on negative and then processing it as a positive. ILM started with that reverse - process footage, and then enhanced it digi tally or — in some cases — created the look from scratch in the computer. During the flashback, a light beam creates a hole in the cabin roof and melts gold coins sitting on a table. The molten gold then flows upwards, out the ceiling opening. Molten gold, achieved as a CG effect by ILM, would be featured more prominently in the film’s third act. As reference, the effects team looked at footage of ferrofluid, a liquid metal that is easily manipulated in the presence of magnetic fields. “We wanted the molten gold to have an otherworldly look,” explained Michael DiComo, “and we thought this ferrofluid was good reference for that. Using magnets, you can almost make ferro
fluid act. You can make it jump from one magnet to another, form into spikes — all kinds of things. So that was the inspiration for these molten gold effects, which were created through painstaking particle effects work done by our effects CG supervisor Dan Pearson and his team. They pulled out all the tricks for that — fluid sims, surface meshes, fields to attract and repel stuff and noise to randomize it. We also did a lot of different render passes and comp treatments to make these ‘fluidy’ renders look like they had a hot core to them; and then we added heat displacement, heat ripples and smoke that had interactive light on it from the bright molten metal.” The makeshift posse continues its search of the aliens, and comes upon the incongruous sight of a capsized riverboat sitting upside-down in the desert. Roger Guyett commissioned New Deal Studios to build a riverboat miniature that he could shoot on location, in the appropriate lighting. “Half the problem of an effects shot is trying to re - create the live - action lighting environment,” asserted Guyett. “So we photo graphed the riverboat model right there on the day, setting up a parallel feed between two cameras so Jon could see the riverboat model superimposed in the background of a shot as we were lining it up.” At New Deal, supervisor Ian Hunter oversaw the build ing of the riverboat miniature. “It was 1/48 - scale,” said Hunter, “which was smaller than what I would have built in normal circumstances. But, in this case, Roger Guyett and one assistant were going to be moving this thing around on location; and so it had to be small enough that two people could carry it from place to place. It was approximately four feet long, and had
a plastic frame inside of it, with urethane foam for the base to keep it lightweight. The hull was covered with individual wooden planks, and the paddlewheel was about 25 percent bigger than scale so you could easily read that it was a riverboat, even from a distance. It was all built intact, at first, and then we cut it up to make it look as if it had been crushed when it impacted the desert.” New Deal surrounded the miniature with ter rain matching the location. “Roger sent us photo graphs of the location, and we matched those with plants, soil and rocks. We added a lot of scaled debris around it, as well, so the visual effects people wouldn’t have to paint in every piece of debris.” ILM’s digimatte team added blowing trees and other bits of life to the static model shots, and also integrated elements to suggest a rainy environment. “None of that feeling of a rainstorm was there in the photography,” noted Eddie Pasquarello. “It was only raining where the actors were, and that was due to sprinklers above them. So we had to add that. Barry Williams did the digimatte work for that shot, and Greg Salter did the composite. It was one of the first shots we started and one of the last we finished.” Production shot riverboat interiors at Uni versal, including a scene in which a young boy, Em mett Taggart (Noah Ringer), is confronted by one of the aliens. Jon Favreau shot the scene with Legacy’s alien; but some of the puppet work was replaced with ILM’s CG alien, largely due to changes in the creature’s design. “We had started with the live - action puppet,” explained Michael DiComo, “but we took it beyond that, both in ani - mation and how it looked. For one thing, Jon Fav reau wanted the aliens to have more battle damage — scars and wounds and that type of thing. That was amaz ing work by our viewpainting team, led by Ron Woodall and Martin Murphy, Paul Giacoppo’s model-
ing team, and the look - dev team, led by Robert Weaver. That creature was under devel - op ment for months, and it continued to evolve until we had these beautifully textured, dirty, grimy and disgusting aliens.” One of the redesigned features was the alien chest cavity that opens to reveal a second pair of arms. “As the cavity opens and the little arms emerge,” explained Eddie Pasquarello, “Jon wanted to see the body plates separating with a tremendous level of complexity — a lot of weird elastic membranes along the join lines, like a strange clam opening. The prac tical alien just wasn’t capable of doing those very complex movements. For the same reason, we replaced the little arms that come out, giving them more subtle movements.” Closeups of the alien puppet’s head remained in the final cut of the film, although some were digitally en hanced by Shade VFX. “We added drool and spittle and breath and steam elements coming off the alien,” said Shade visual effects supervisor Bryan Godwin, “all to make the alien look more menacing, particularly in a tight two - shot with the boy, where the alien is right up in his face. At one point, the alien roars, and we added strands of gooey stuff as the mouth opened, using a cloth simulation inside Maya.” Other closeups featured ILM’s CG alien, the animation of which — headed up by ani mator Derek Carlin — was based on the facial expressions achieved with the puppet. “Jon didn’t want it to be obvious when he was cutting from the CG alien to the practical alien,” Marc Chu commented. “He wanted that hand - off to be seamless. So we dialed down our alien facial shapes to make the animation match what had been done with
the puppet. Once the transition to CG was made, we could go a little bit further with it.” The boy survives his encounter with the alien and rejoins the posse. As the band rides across the desert, speeders come around a butte and begin rounding up and firing on it. The Embassy executed the initial shot of the approaching speeders. “There are riders and horses in front,” related Winston Hel ga - son, “and then four speeders fly in and strafe the characters below, and six or seven huge explosions go off. There were only two practical explosions in that plate; and so, we designed a digital explosion in Hou dini that was a 99 - percent perfect match to the prac tical explosion. We ended up using that in a lot of different shots to beef up the sequences.” The Embassy’s work on the shots, in addition to blending the speeders and explosions into the plates, included animating digital horses and riders being impacted by weapons fire. “It’s a PG -13 movie,” stated Helgason, “so we couldn’t go too far with this stuff; but there are several shots of riders being blown off horses. There was also a lot of stuff of people being lifted off their horses with the bolo hooks. In the plates, there were guys on horses with a cable attached to them, pulling them off the animals. We made the transition from that to our digital doubles, and replaced the cable with our illuminated bolo.” Ella is abducted during the melee, lifted into the air via bolo — a practical stunt that Olivia Wilde performed herself, on location. Jake, on horseback, gives chase alongside the speeder carrying Ella; and when the aircraft dives into a canyon, Jake leaps from his
horse atop a cliff, lands on the speeder, and finally disables it with his blaster, causing it to crash into a lake. “When we first saw the boards for that sequence,” recalled Eddie Pasquarello, “we knew it was going to be a big undertaking to make it believable. It was another one of those great cross - genre things, because Jake deals with this alien aircraft the way he would deal with a bull or a runaway horse. He mounts the speeder and uses his raw cowboy instincts to bring it down.” Production shot the actors on a gimbaled speeder at an outdoor area of Universal Studios. “Matthew Liba tique and I were keen on photographing that in real sun light,” recalled Roger Guyett. “Unfortunately, we ended up shooting it during three of the foggiest, most overcast days I’d seen in L.A. for years! But at least we had natural light for a lot of that stuff.” Legacy Effects provided the practical speeder for Dan Sudick’s gimbal setup, which was like a massive steel spit to which the speeder could be mounted to facilitate 360 - degree rolls or, if mounted at different points, vertical configurations. In addition, the wings were hydraulically controlled to move up and down. To create a sense of high speed, the actors would be safety - wired to the vertically - positioned speeder as giant fans blew on them. ILM and The Embassy realized speeder action that could not be achieved with the actors on the practical rig. ILM did the initial wide shot of Jake and Ella on the speeder, and The Embassy took over the scene from there. “We did several shots that had a digital stunt double of Ella underneath the speeder and a digital stunt double of Jake on top,” said Winston Helgason. ”ILM gave us Jake and Ella assets so that we could switch
to their digital doubles when necessary.” The speeder’s crash into the lake was an all - CG shot, as were subsequent shots of the alien pilot emerging from the water. “When the alien falls back into the water,” said Eddie Pasquarello, “that was a stuntman wearing a gray suit, which gave us the initial displacement. We were able to salvage some of the water that kicked off of him. And then we also had a stuntman that jumped out of the water to give us reference; but the water that flowed off of him was unusable, and so we had to do that with CG effects.” Ella is mauled by the alien pilot and subsequently dies, her body taken to an Indian encampment and ceremoniously placed into a fire. Within mo ments, a vision of Ella begins to appear, seemingly formed out of the flames. Fuel VFX, in Aus tralia, took on the stand - alone sequence. “It was one of those open artistic briefs,” said Fuel visual effects supervisor Simon Maddison. “They wanted it to look ethereal, and yet it still had to be grounded in reality. It couldn’t feel too magical. That was the balance we were always trying to find — how to make this ethereal and alien, and yet real enough that you be lieved it was happening.” Fuel started with plates that had real fire in them. “We pushed the live - action fire around to see if we could get the right look that way,” said Maddison, “but it was easier to do it all CG because we could make it move exactly the way we wanted. The fire in the plates was still very useful, because it gave us reference for how our fire should look. Ours just had to move differently. It was also useful because we were able to keep the interactive light from it, although we added some of that, as well, to reflect when
our fire got bigger and smaller, or danced to the left or the right.” Fuel used Maya’s fluid simulation to generate the foundation of the digital fire, and then built on top of that with custom particle systems. “The Maya fluid solver is fantastic,” said Maddison, “but it is slow and cumbersome. So we used the Maya fluid solve to generate the base campfire, which was the Maya fluid sim, and got that doing a certain thing; and then we added the particle stuff, which was also driven by the fluid sim. So the base fire took a long time to turn around, but we could see versions of the par - ticle ‘magic fire’ fairly quickly.” More difficult than the CG fire was creating Ella’s image being formed in its flames. Fuel introduced an Ella digital double, provided by ILM, into its CG fire imagery, doing many different iterations before arriving at the appropriately ethereal look. “We started by putting the digital double into the live - action plate, inside the fire,” noted Maddison, “but that just looked like a girl on fire. The fire was meant to be form ing her. How do you do that? We used negative space to show that she was physically there, even before you clearly see her; but if we used too much negative space, it looked like a hole in the fire. Then we created versions of the digital double that were actually made of fire; but too much of that and it just blended in, making it very hard to see her shape. Finding her form inside the fire was quite challenging, and involved trying this and that until we got it.” To make the digital double’s hair blow around as Ella is formed, Maddison shot a stand - in in front of fans. “I shot her hair and we put that on the digital double, rather than coming up with a whole hair system, which seemed like overkill for a few shots.”
Ella explains her remarkable resurrection, informing Jake and the others that she is an alien, as well — the sole survivor of a people from a distant planet who had previously fallen prey to the aliens. Shade contributed shots suggesting Jake’s transition into an altered consciousness, working closely with Jon Favreau and Roger Guyett to determine the look. “We worked with them to come up with something that looked trippy, as if he was being transported into an other world,” said Bryan Godwin. “We had many rounds of creative design, and we went all the way from crazy tie - dyed effects to what’s in the film, which involved incorporating footage of the alien light we see later that turns abductees into zombies. You can’t really tell that’s what it is, but Favreau wanted to tie that element to this flashback sequence. We developed the effect in Nuke, using layers of elements from other shots in the movie to hint at what was happening to the aliens’ victims.” Fuel animated a hummingbird — a manifestation of the spirit of Alice — that ap pears during Jake’s flashback. “Just before he starts remembering,” said Mad dison, “this hum - mingbird flies through the top of the tent, lands on his finger and then takes off again. Jon Favreau wanted it to be an authentic hummingbird from that area of New Mexico, and that’s what we referenced. We built and rigged the model and, since we weren’t sure how close they were going to get to it, built a feather system for it, as well.” In the flashback sequence, we see Jake on an operat ing table, about to be dissected by what came to be called the ‘über - alien,’ conceived as a more sophis ti cated being that conducts experiments on human ab duc tees within the bowels of the alien ship. “We
wanted a slightly different alien that would be one notch higher on the evolutionary scale,” Roger Guyett noted, “and so he’s got a slightly different design. He still shares some of the features of the other aliens — like the baby arms that come out of his chest — but the shape of his head and the quality of his skin are different. Robert Weaver and sequence supervisor Tom Fejes took the lead in developing the look of the über - alien.” “A lot of ideas were thrown out there as to what this über - alien should look like,” added Robert Weaver. “The final result is an alien that has less of a slope to his forehead to make him look more intelligent. His jaw line is more pronounced, and his teeth are like sharks’ teeth. We drew on sea creatures a lot in the design of him. He has little gills behind his eye, where an ear would be. Jon Favreau also wanted it to have a trans lu - cent quality, like a soft - shelled crab. So he has translucency that is similar to the other aliens, but without the harder shell parts — because he doesn’t go out and fight in the battles. He stays in the ship burrowed in the caves.” To create a sense of depth in the über - alien’s translucent flesh, ILM modelers built two to three layers of skin, depending on the area of the body. “The idea was that you’d see through the first layer. and then most of the textural detail would be in the second layer. He also had internal organs that you would see through the translucent skin.” The über - alien’s animation, too — especially his facial animation — would differ from that of the ‘grunt’ alien. “This alien has a beef with Jake because of Jake’s earlier attack on him and escape from the operating room,” explained Marc Chu. “Jon Favreau wanted to see that this guy is pissed off and looking for revenge later on, when he comes into contact with Jake again. So we had to come up with a whole facial library just so
this one special alien could emote and play off of the other characters. Ani mator Peter Kelly came up with the attitude and emotional content of the über - alien so you felt that connection between him and Jake.” Through the flashback, Jake recalls the location of the aliens’ mining operation and mothership, and leads the posse there. Arriving at the site, the band finds an alien ship that is half - buried beneath the desert floor, the other half a massive tower jutting skyward. Production built a 30 - foot - tall base of the structure on location for actor interaction with the tower. For distant full views, Roger Guyett hired New Deal Studios to create a tower miniature that he could shoot in the actual location, using that photography as the basis for the many subsequent battle shots in which the full CG tower would be in the background. “With the onslaught of the battle and explosions going off all over the place, and all the smoke and dust that would be there,” explained Guyett, “the last thing we wanted was to end up with a bunch of really complicated composites just to put the tower in the background. Know ing that we were going to be quite a distance from the tower in a lot of those shots, I thought that if we could build a reasonably accurate miniature, we could position it out on location, and every time we photographed a setup, we would photograph the miniature from the same angles and register those images with that particular setup. I thought that would give us a good starting point in terms of lighting for any work we did later. If the miniature was good enough and we were diligent enough about taking the photographs, we could almost cut that thing out and paste it into the image, and we’d have a good starting
point for how the tower would really look out there. It was a good idea in theory; but unfortunately, the weather changed dramatically when we were out there, lit er ally minute to minute, and in evitably the photo graphs we took wound up being shot under different lighting conditions. In some cases, though, we were able to use them as relatively simple elements within the shots.” New Deal built two versions of the alien tower — a finished miniature that would be photographed to pull tex tures for ILM’s matte - painted digital tower, and a smaller gray model used strictly for lighting reference. “We rapid - prototyped the gray model,” explained Ian Hunter, “taking the digital file from ILM’s CG model, and growing it in ABS resin. The finished model was 1/32 scale, measuring about four feet tall and 30 inches at the base. The biggest challenge was just matching both the practical tower base they’d built on location and ILM’s digital model, because there were discrepancies between the digital model and the set photographs. We had to make sure that our model matched the full - size set in terms of texture, but matched the digital model for shape.” On location, Roger Guyett and a crew member positioned the tower miniature for each setup. “We carried this four - foot model of the tower around with us like a chalice,” Guyett recalled. “It was great lighting reference; and if we were lucky, we could just composite the picture into the final shot, which gave us a starting point for the digimatte tower. Even when the miniature tower was entirely replaced, it gave us good lighting reference for how the tower would look out there.” Final shots of the alien tower were divided between ILM and Ghost, a Danish com-
pany. To earn the assignment, Ghost had submitted a test shot in which the camera tilts up to reveal the huge ship tower in front of Jake in the foreground. On the basis of that successful test, the company was awarded 69 shots, 39 of which made their way into the final cut. “The main element in our shots was the tower itself,” said Ghost visual effects supervisor Jeppe Nygaard Christensen. “We got the model from ILM, added to the existing textures, and ren dered it to place it into the environment. We were sup plied with matte balls and chrome balls and the miniature photography from location. So we would make a CG gray ball and render that to make it look exactly like the real gray ball. If we matched that lighting and the lighting on the miniature, we knew we were close to where we needed to be.” To create a sense of atmosphere, Ghost added smoke belching from the tower, elements created using Fume FX. Some of the alien tower’s features and surface detail evolved at ILM as postproduction progressed, requiring digital changes not only to the 3D tower, but to 2D images of the practical set piece, as well. “Jon Favreau didn’t love the look of the tower set once he saw it on film,” observed Michael DiComo. “So we had to change that — remaining true to what was in the plate, but cleaning it up and slightly changing the surface so it didn’t look so much like a molded piece. We gave it a slicker, more high - tech finish, adding more engineering and moving parts.” With the new directive, Ghost animated moving features such as pistons, gears and lights to create a sense of life in its tower shots, and also resurfaced the prac tical set piece. “We repainted the tower manually and composited surface treatment throughout to get the specific look that Jon Favreau wanted,” said Jeppe Nygaard Christensen.
“There were bubbly cement artifacts that we had to get rid of; and then we added other surface treatments, such as soil running down the sides.” Ghost also built the impact zone surrounding the tower. “We built rocks jutting out of the ground, as well as the funnel that has been carved into the earth by the tower. We had conceptual sketches from ILM to show us what that should look like.” After enlisting reinforcements from members of his old gang, Jake returns to the tower site and, with Dolarhyde, leads the small army of cowboys and Indians in a low - tech war against the aliens. The ‘cowboys versus aliens’ battle begins with the men climbing up the side of the tower to deposit saddlebags filled with dynamite. Pro duction shot the actors scaling the partial tower set on location for the beginning of their climb; and then, for shots of them reaching the top, production used an elevated set piece built at Universal. “That tower piece was about 50 - by - 50 - feet,” explained Roger Guyett. “We either shot it with the actors against sky, which we could often do because they were elevated off the ground, or we brought a bluescreen in. Other times, we’d shoot it as a postage - stamp piece that we could drop back into the whole tower to give it scale. We’d photograph the actors climbing the set piece, and then cut that out and stick it on the CG tower model.” Ghost contributed three subsequent shots of dynamite explosions. “A lot of the flames and debris and smoke elements in that were CG,” said Jeppe Nygaard Christ - ensen, “but there was also a practical explosion element. We added fire and smoke elements to that fireball to make a seamless interaction between it and the speeder that
emerges from it, and also animated the speeder itself, using ILM’s asset. It was only three shots, but we were quite honored that ILM let us animate the speeder in those shots.” Ghost further sweetened the shot by adding flames, smoke trails and flying debris surrounding the expelled speeder. With the massive dynamite blasts, aliens begin swarming out of the tower, attacking Jake and his men on the ground as speeders strafe the area from above. Shooting live - action for the chaotic battle sequence — which would ultimately be populated by CG aliens that were conspicuously absent on location — was a major logis tical challenge. “There were a lot of elements that made shooting that sequence complicated,” said Roger Guyett. “For one thing, we shot the movie anamorphic; and with anamorphic, the image exposes exactly as you see it, with no up or down or left or right. So we often shot ref erence tiles just in case we wanted to do a change of some kind — how much we were tilting or panning, for example. We shot reference stills around the shot itself so we’d have some latitude to change the shot later. “Beyond that, just getting what we needed in the plate was very difficult. We were extremely limited as to what kind of stunt work we could do with the horses, for one thing, and we always had issues with the explosions, especially around the animals. And, as always when dealing with a lot of CG characters, it was very hard to get a representation of those characters in the scene that everyone could get their heads around. If you have an alien running across a wide shot, and you’ve got four different extras on horseback shooting at him, where is he at any given point? It sounds like a trivial problem, but it’s not at all. We did the classic thing, which was to have gray - suited guys
standing in for the aliens; but since the aliens would be running faster than a human being could, that was not a perfect solution, either.” By the end of the shoot, all Favreau had for his film’s epic final battle was live - action footage of individual action vignettes, stunt gags, practical pyro - technics, and the gray - suited stand - ins running around the location. To turn those bits of live - action into a fully realized battle scene, he was putting his faith in ILM — and, at first, his faith was a little shaky. “This was a battle sequence with multiple CGI characters,” Favreau said. “On top of that, I had always been taught to set this kind of stuff at night, and here we were doing our big closing se quence in daylight. I had just gotten comfortable with CG hard surfaces with Iron Man. But this time I was getting into biological characters, and alien biological characters, at that. So we were trying to create something that wasn’t real in the first place, and make it feel real in a daylight environment — while interacting with humans and horses. That was tremendously complicated compared to anything I’d done with ILM before, and it made me nervous.” “On the set,” Roger Guyett said, “Jon would look at me, and say, ‘Are you going to be able to make this look good?’ I told him, ‘Jon, you’ll be surprised.’ And when he saw the first exterior shot we did with an alien, he was blown away by it. He said, ‘Okay, I’m not worried anymore.’ The alien looked completely real in those environments; and that was the result of very hard work by everybody at ILM.” A crucial component of that work was executed by ILM’s effects team, which generated volumes of dust and dirt elements to marry the CG aliens to the desert environ-
ment. “We pride ourselves in making a CG character live within the environment,” observed Michael DiComo, “and part of that is getting the environment onto the creature, making sure that if it’s standing in the sand, it’s interacting with the sand. So throughout the battle scenes, the aliens had to be kicking up sand and dirt and dust. That involved classic particle effects; but because there was so much of it, the challenge was to stream line our typical processes so that pretty much any TD could run those without having to be a particle expert.” Wounds sustained by the aliens in the course of battle would also tie them to the live - action. “We had a rig set up so that arrows, for example, could penetrate anywhere on the alien’s body,” said Robert Weaver, “and immediately upon contact a wound would appear. We wrote shaders to give the aliens different wounds and scars and gouges, so they wouldn’t all appear to be the same creature. In terms of markings, there were three variations of the regular alien, and then a fourth that could be dismembered and blown apart in the course of the battle.” While the battle rages outside, Jake and Ella make their way into the caverns housing the subterranean section of the ship, their intention to find and destroy its core. Ella removes the blaster from Jake’s wrist, and then crawls up into the higher levels of the ship. The digimatte team provided Ella’s vertiginous point of view as she looks down through multiple levels of catacomb- like rock formations. “She was shot bluescreen on the little triangular piece she comes out on,” said Michael DiComo. “That’s the only thing that was photographed — everything else was just bluescreen, and ILM had to fill in the rest of the environment.”
While Ella makes her way toward the ship’s core, Jake is captured and pinned to an examination table by the über - alien. Effects lead Raul Essig led the development of molten gold effects related to the über - alien’s demise. Inside the ship, Ella makes her way to the core and uses the blaster to destroy it, ensuring her own destruction in the subsequent massive aerial explosion. Roger Guyett shot pyrotechnics at Universal Studios for some of the initial explosions within the ship, but the 200 - foot fireball that blasts through the interior, and the fol low - ing exterior shots of the ship’s explosion, were real ized by the digital effects team at ILM. “That technology has come a long way in the past couple of years,” said Guyett. “The level of control is much better than it used to be, and the fire element of explosions is some thing we can do very well now. That’s not to say that there isn’t a place for pyrotechnics — there clearly is. In the town attack, for example, most of the pyro elements were live, and we got great lighting and shadowing and interaction from them. But it is very hard, even with a miniature, to create a 200 - foot fireball.” Always a fan of the in - camera effect, Jon Favreau had considered shooting models for both interior and exterior views of the tower explosion, an approach Guyett did not

I agreed to investigate that, but I also wanted to do a CG test. So Lee Uren and Dan Pearson did a test where a CG fireball moved through the interior of the ship; and when Jon looked at that the first time, he thought it was a model element. He said, ‘Oh, so you shot something already?’ And I said, ‘No, this is a CG test.’ He was amazed.” The climactic shot of the ship’s explosion in midair evolved throughout postproduction, moving from a basic physics - based simulated explosion to one with a little more movie magic. “There was a strong narrative throughout the movie that the aliens are mining for gold,” related Guyett, “and we realized that we needed to account for all of that gold that was in the ship when it blows up. So we added a cloud of spar kling gold that surrounds the ship after the explosion, along with all the billowing Plume smoke.” The film ends with the aliens defeated and Jake leaving town in classic western fashion — alone, save for his horse. With Cowboys & Aliens, Jon Fav reau and his collaborators pulled off the neat trick of artfully blending two distinct film genres, making believ able the fantastic conceit of technologically ad vanced aliens invading an iconic American West world. Through out, Favreau brought to the process a love and admiration of classic sci - fi and westerns past, and he infused Cowboys & Aliens with elements of both — but with enough 21st - century originality and punch to appeal to a new generation of moviegoers. “Ultimately,” Favreau concluded, “all I could do was make a movie that I would want
to see, something that would deliver as a big, popcorn film, and yet be original. People want to escape to the movies, and part of that is being walked through stories that are familiar to them emotionally; but they also want to see something new, to be surprised. Using technology, we were able to apply those old stories to well - worn genres, but in a new and hopefully surprising way. That’s always what filmmakers have done with new technology. “I’m reading a biography of Walt Disney now, and it’s clear that animation was groundbreaking. Squash and stretch, shading, the way they treated ink and paint — all of it, groundbreaking. And reading about the expense and trouble they went through to develop it, and the debates they had about it, it sounds just like the conversations about CGI. ‘It’s so expensive! We’re running out of time, and we need a release date! Why are we pushing for this technology? Why don’t we just stick with what we have?’ It’s always been the same when a new technology comes along.
